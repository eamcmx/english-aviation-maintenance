Latvian & Russian interference — expanded

These are the errors to expect from Latvian (L1) and Russian (L1) speakers in this unit. Each note says why it happens, so you can recast with a reason rather than just correcting.

1 · The doubled "to": "runs to P1 to J12" → "runs from P1 to J12"

Why. Latvian and Russian mark direction with grammatical case endings and a small set of prepositions (LV no … līdz; RU от … до / из … в), and learners over-generalise a single directional marker onto English "to". The idea "there is a start and an end" is carried by the noun form, not by two contrasting words, so English's obligatory from … to pairing is easy to miss. Recast: "from the start, to the end" with a hand gesture left-to-right.

2 · "in" a pin/connector: "the contact is in pin 3" → "on pin 3 / at pin 3"

Why. Russian «в» and Latvian iekšā/pie map imperfectly onto English in/on/at. A pin feels like a container to the learner ("the wire is inside the connector"), so «в» → "in" is transferred. English treats a pin as a point/surface: a contact sits on or at it. Recast: touch a fingertip to a surface — "on the pin, at the pin", never "in".

3 · "trough" + dropped article: "goes trough harness" → "through the harness"

Why. Two problems combine. (a) Neither Latvian nor Russian has articles, so the is routinely dropped before a known noun. (b) through is hard: the <th> sound is absent in both L1s and the <ough> spelling is opaque, so learners write the more familiar word "trough". Recast: model the sound /θruː/ and the article — "through the harness"; point to the spelling on the board.

Other predictable slips

  • Consonant clusters & endings: "continuty", "resistence" — the unstressed vowel is dropped or misheard. Drill by syllable: con-tin-u-i-ty, re-sist-ance.
  • /v/–/w/ confusion: "wire / voltage" — Latvian and Russian have no /w/; both may surface as [v]. Not an error to over-correct, but flag it for the read-back task where clarity matters.
  • Third-person -s and articles in the STE writing — expect "the wire run" and missing "a/the"; STE's short imperative style actually helps here (Traced… Inspected…).
  • "short circuit" vs "open circuit" mapped to the wrong symptom — a conceptual, not linguistic, slip; the meter widget fixes it fast.

Extension activities

  • Blind reference dictation (fast finishers). One learner reads six references from a card (e.g. "P4, pin 7, top row"); the partner writes them and reads them back. Any mismatch = redo. Trains the read-back safety habit under speed.
  • Draw-the-path. Give a spoken route ("from P1 pin 3, along the loom, into J12 socket B, ground on pin 4"); learners sketch it on paper, then compare with the tracer widget. Reverse it: sketch → describe.
  • Fault-or-not sorting. Read out ten meter readings ("infinite", "0.2 ohms", "near zero to ground", "OL"…); learners call "open", "short", or "good". Builds the listening-for-figures skill the quiz tests.
  • STE rewrite race. Give a heavy, comma-spliced work-order sentence; pairs rewrite it into short single-idea STE steps against the clock. Compare versions on the board.
  • Handover chain. Learner A leaves a 30-second spoken handover (voicemail style); B listens once and writes the two outstanding jobs; C acts them out. Mirrors role-play card 4.
  • AI partner homework. Point learners to the three chat-partner prompts in the lesson for extra rehearsal of locate / explain-an-open / handover, at their own pace.
