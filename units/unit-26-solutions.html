E1 Gap-fill: use a bank word

#AnswerWhy
1dentA dent is the smooth hollow an impact pushes into the skin — no split, no sharp edge.
2stringerA stringer runs lengthwise and stiffens the skin from the inside.
3allowable damage limitThis is the largest damage the manual permits before a repair becomes necessary.
4referralWhen damage is too big to fix locally, a referral sends the case to engineering.
5corrosionCorrosion is chemical, rust-like metal damage — the only bank word for a chemical process.
6blend outTo blend out is to sand away light surface damage gently; "blend it out" is also accepted.
7within limitsIf depth stays under the maximum, the damage is within limits, so no repair is needed.
8dent depthThe depth gauge measures the dent depth — how far the hollow goes into the skin.
9zoneA zone is a numbered area that locates the work; it answers "which area?".
10stationA station is a numbered position measured along the fuselage length.

TermLetterWhy

gougeFA deeper mark than a scratch; it removes more metal.

E6 Error-spotting: fix the report lines

#Wrong → RightWhy
1which → whoA mechanic is a person; "which" is only for things.
2who → which (or that)A dent is a thing, so it takes "which" or "that", never "who".
3gouge → gaugeThe tool is a depth gauge; a gouge is a deep mark — near-homophones.
4with → withinThe fixed phrase is "within limits"; "with limits" is not English here.
5scratsh → scratchSpelling: "scratch" ends in -tch.
6which → whereA zone is a place, so the relative word is "where".
7that → whichAfter a comma (non-defining) you cannot use "that"; use "which".
8lock → logMeasurements go in the tech log, the record book, not a "lock".

Teaching note: the trap is depth vs location. On flat skin 1.4 mm would be within limits, but this dent is over a stringer, where the limit drops to 1.0 mm — so the same number becomes out of limits. Location decides which limit applies.

WRITE Model answer — Ground Damage Report (annotated)

An 80–120 word Simplified Technical English report for the Dialogue 3 dent. It is deliberately plain: short sentences, one idea per line, the active voice, and only common words plus this unit's specialist terms.

GROUND DAMAGE REPORT 1. What happened: A tug struck the upper fuselage during towing. 2. Damage: one dent in the skin. No crack. No corrosion. 3. Location: zone 300, station 720, over a frame. 4. Measurements: depth 2.6 mm, length 35 mm, width 25 mm. 5. SRM check: the allowable damage limit over a frame is 1.0 mm. 6. Result: the dent depth is 2.6 mm. The dent is out of limits. 7. Recommendation: raise a referral to engineering. Engineering must design a repair scheme. 8. Action: the aircraft is on hold. Do not fit the panel. Reported by: [name]. Date: 08 AUG 2026. (96 words)

Why this answer scores well

  • Location before extent. Line 3 fixes the damage by zone and station and notes it is over a frame — the reader knows which limit applies before any number appears.
  • The comparison is explicit. Line 5 states the limit (1.0 mm) and line 6 states the measurement (2.6 mm), so the "out of limits" verdict is visibly justified, not asserted.
  • Recommendation with a reason. Line 7 recommends a referral and gives the reason in the next clause: engineering must design the scheme because the SRM does not cover a frame.
  • STE discipline. One idea per numbered line; active voice ("A tug struck…", "raise a referral"); no long words. This is exactly what the writing task asks for.
  • Safe close. The last action line puts the aircraft on hold and blocks the panel fit — the safety instruction the next shift needs first.
Common learner slips to correct: writing "it is out of limit" (fixed phrase = out of limits); passive drift ("the fuselage was struck by a tug" — STE prefers the active "a tug struck the fuselage"); and giving the depth without the limit, which hides the reason for the verdict.
