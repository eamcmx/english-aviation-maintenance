First-language interference — Latvian & Russian

These learners share two features that shape their English errors: no articles, and a subjunctive/conditional built with a single particle. Anticipate the errors below and recast them gently.

1 · "Would" in the if-clause of a conditional

If we would have ordered the part, we would have it now.

If we had ordered the part, we would have it now.

Why it happens: Latvian ("ja mēs būtu pasūtījuši") and Russian ("если бы мы заказали") mark the whole conditional with one particle (būtu / бы) that appears in both clauses. Learners map that onto English "would" and put it in both halves. English splits the two clauses: had + past participle in the if-clause, would only in the result.

2 · Dropped articles "the" and "a"

Pump is no-go item, so we cannot defer defect.

The pump is a no-go item, so we cannot defer the defect.

Why it happens: neither Latvian nor Russian has articles; definiteness is shown by word order and context. Learners simply have no habit of inserting "the/a". Drill the rule "specific known thing → the; one of many → a" on the exact unit nouns (the MEL, a spare, the tech log).

3 · "was" for "were", and present simple in the result

If the part was in stock, we fix it now.

If the part were in stock, we would fix it now.

Why it happens: the unreal "were" for all persons is idiomatic and has no clean equivalent in the L1s, so learners default to "was". They also translate the result as a plain present ("мы чиним" / "mēs labojam") instead of "would + verb". Recast both parts together.

4 · Bonus: consonant clusters & word stress

Watch showstopper (three stressed syllables become flat), sign-off (silent "g"), and the letter names in AOG / MEL / IPC / BITE — Russian speakers often voice final consonants and Latvian speakers shorten long vowels. Drill the stress pattern of "no-go item".

Extension activities (fast finishers / a second session)

  • Chain drill. In fours, pass the same defect up the whole chain (mechanic → technician → supervisor → manager) in four 60-second briefings. Each speaker must simplify for the next listener.
  • Register switch. Give one technical sentence; students rewrite it twice — once for another engineer, once for the non-technical duty manager. Compare in pairs.
  • STE clean-up. Hand out a wordy, real-sounding defect note; students cut it to Simplified Technical English (one idea per sentence, approved words, under 18 words).
  • What-if review. Students write three mixed conditionals about tonight's decisions ("If stores had held a spare, …"). Peer-check for "would" in the if-clause.
  • Voicemail back. Students record a 30-second AOG update voicemail to Maintenance Control, then swap and transcribe a partner's, checking the numbers.

CEFR B2 speaking rubric — for the four role-plays

Score each learner 1–5 on the five criteria. B2 sits at bands 3–4. Use it to give one focus per role-play, not five corrections.

BandRangeAccuracyFluencyInteractionCoherence
5 · above B2 Uses the unit terms and flexible phrasing to brief technical and non-technical listeners with ease. High control; mixed conditionals and articles almost always correct; slips are self-repaired. Smooth, natural pace; hesitation only to plan content, not to find words. Leads the escalation, checks understanding, and adapts to the listener without prompting. Clear problem → constraint → ask → plan structure with natural connectors.
4 · strong B2 Good range of unit vocabulary; some reformulation to explain a term to a non-engineer. Generally accurate; occasional article or conditional error that does not block meaning. Mostly fluent; a few pauses but keeps going. Responds well, asks and answers, reaches the goal (no-go, escalate, plan). Logical order; uses "so", "because", "unless", "as soon as" appropriately.
3 · B2 threshold Enough vocabulary for the task; leans on fixed phrases from the cards. Errors present (articles dropped, "would" in if-clause) but meaning is clear. Some slow patches; occasional restart, still communicative. Takes part and reaches the goal with a little support from the partner. Order mostly clear; connectors simple but correct.
2 · below B2 Limited vocabulary; frequent gaps that stop the briefing. Frequent errors that sometimes block meaning; no self-repair. Frequent long pauses; effortful. Mostly reactive; relies on the partner to drive. Ideas listed rather than linked; jumps around.
1 · well below Very few words; cannot sustain the role. Errors in almost every utterance; meaning often lost. Halting; long silences. Cannot keep the exchange going. No clear structure.

Target evidence for a B2 pass: the learner states it is a no-go item, chooses to escalate, gives a lead time, and agrees a recovery plan — while adapting language for the non-technical manager in Card 3.
