Reading a wiring diagram and connector/pin talk

A conductor is only useful if you can find it again. In this unit you learn to read a wiring diagram, name a connector and a pin with no ambiguity, describe the exact path of a wire, and explain a suspected open or short circuit to a colleague, the way a Part-66 avionics technician does it on the line.

SpecialismAvionics & electrical, EASA Part-66/145 LevelCEFR B2 · self-study, 90 minutes Grammar focusPrepositions of position and path Writing habitASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English

By the end of this unit you can …

  • Can locate a wire, connector and pin on a WDM and describe the path.
  • Can communicate pin and connector references without ambiguity.
  • Can explain a suspected open or short circuit to a colleague.

01The situation

You are working a landing-gear warning fault at a Part-145 line station this afternoon. The gear-down lamp stays off during the extension check, so the flight crew has written the snag into the tech log. Your task is to trace the affected conductor on the wiring diagram, which is the master drawing of every wire in the aircraft. You must then locate the correct connector and pin on a mock harness, and describe the route out loud to your partner. One technician reads the diagram while the other locates the parts and repeats each reference back. Unless both people use identical words, they inevitably disconnect the wrong wire, so precise connector and pin talk is a genuine safety task rather than casual conversation.

Dialogue 1: Tracing a conductor on the WDM

Transcript

ARight, pull up the wiring diagram for the landing-gear warning, because the gear-down lamp stays off.

BIt is on the screen now, although the drawing is crowded here; which conductor are we chasing?

AWire W241, which originates at pin 3 on connector P1, near the avionics rack.

BUnderstood, but from P1 the route splits, so where exactly does the conductor terminate?

AIt travels through the harness behind the instrument panel, and then continues onward to connector J12.

BSo the routing is P1 pin 3, along the loom, and finally into connector J12.

AExactly right; now read me the socket number where the conductor lands inside J12.

BThe diagram clearly indicates socket B on J12, immediately beside the shielded pair.

AGood, because that particular contact feeds the gear-down warning lamp in the cockpit.

BAnd the return path? I still need to know where the ground reference actually sits.

AThe ground is on pin 4, connected by a bonding strap to the airframe structure.

BUnderstood, so unless the ground connection is broken, the warning lamp should illuminate normally.

ACorrect, although as soon as continuity breaks anywhere, the lamp immediately goes dark.

BIn that case we should test continuity from P1 through to J12 before anything else.

AAgreed; grab the multimeter, select the continuity range, and back-probe the plug carefully.

BOn it, and I will also read the receptacle end so that we compare both connectors.

Dialogue 3: Explaining an open or short circuit

Transcript

AThe gear lamp stays completely off, so I strongly suspect we have an open circuit.

BWhat exactly makes you say it is open, rather than a short somewhere?

AThe multimeter reads no continuity at all between connector P1 and connector J12.

BSo the electrical path is broken somewhere along the conductor between those points.

APrecisely; the resistance reads infinite, which normally confirms an open circuit.

BCould a backed-out pin inside the connector realistically cause a reading like that?

AIt certainly could, because a loose contact interrupts continuity just as effectively.

BAnd a short circuit instead? How would that appear differently on the meter?

AA short reads near zero resistance to ground, rather than the infinite reading we see.

BBecause the exposed conductor touches the airframe through damaged insulation somewhere.

AExactly, and in that situation the shield itself may be grounding the inner core.

BSo first we inspect the whole harness carefully for any chafing or damage.

AThen we methodically re-seat every pin inside the plug, one contact at a time.

BAnd if the splice at the terminal block feels loose, we carefully re-make it.

ARight, because unless we actually locate the open, the warning lamp stays dead.

BUnderstood; I will write the whole investigation up in the tech log immediately.

03Vocabulary bank

These are the taught specialist terms for this unit. Each entry gives a plain meaning, one example sentence at your level, and one common word pair, called a collocation. Learn the collocation together with the term, because that is how the words actually appear in real maintenance documentation.

pinA small male metal contact inside a connector.Wire W241 terminates on pin 3, which is the third contact inside connector P1.pin number
socketThe female cavity that receives a pin.The socket receives a pin whenever the two connector halves mate together.socket contact

04Grammar in context, prepositions of position and path

Pin routing must be spatially exact, because a single wrong preposition sends your colleague to entirely the wrong place. For that reason we describe the position of a contact and the path of a conductor with real precision.

Look carefully at these lines taken directly from the dialogues you have just heard:

  • "Wire W241 originates from pin 3 on connector P1." (this gives the start point and the position)
  • "It travels through the harness, and then continues to J12." (this gives the path and the end point)
  • "It runs from P4, down the loom, to terminal block TB2." (this describes the complete path)
  • "The ground reference sits on pin 4." (this gives a fixed position)

Every conductor has three parts you must describe: a start, a path, and an end. Use from for the start, a path word for the middle section, and to for the end. For a fixed position on a component, English prefers at or on rather than in.

PrepositionUseExample
fromthe start of the pathThe conductor runs from connector P1.
tothe end of the pathIt continues to connector J12.
throughpassing inside somethingIt travels through the harness.
at / ona fixed position on a partThe contact sits at pin 3, on the terminal block.

Other path words you will frequently need include: along the loom, into the connector, out of the plug, between pin 3 and pin 4, behind the panel, and next to the clamp.

Three common mistakes made by Latvian and Russian speakers

✗ "The conductor runs to P1 to J12."
✓ "The conductor runs from P1 to J12."
A described path needs a start and an end, so use from for the start rather than repeating to.
✗ "The contact is in pin 3."
✓ "The contact is on pin 3." (or "at pin 3")
The Russian preposition "в" pulls learners towards in, but a contact sits on or at a pin.
✗ "It goes trough harness to J12."
✓ "It goes through the harness to J12."
The correct word is through, never "trough", and English needs the article the before harness.

E5 · Reading 1 (AMM task card)

Read the maintenance task card, then answer the questions

AMM 32-61-00 · Task card 4 · Gear-down warning, fault isolation

The purpose of this task is to isolate the gear-down warning fault systematically. First, open the wiring diagram for the landing-gear warning system and locate wire W241. This conductor originates at pin 3 on connector P1 and terminates at socket B on connector J12. Set the multimeter to the continuity range, and then disconnect both P1 and J12 before probing. Back-probe the plug at connector P1, and measure the continuity carefully from pin 3 through to socket B. A serviceable conductor reads a very low resistance, whereas an open circuit reads no continuity whatsoever. If the reading indicates an open, inspect the harness thoroughly for physical damage along its routing. Check the insulation for chafing wherever the conductor passes near a clamp or a bracket. Afterwards, re-seat each pin inside the connector, and confirm the splice at terminal block TB2. Finally, verify the bonding on pin 4, and record the complete result in the aircraft tech log. Note that you must never pull directly on the conductor during this continuity test.

06Speaking, role-play cards

Work through these cards with a partner, or practise with the AI conversation partner described below. Swap roles after each card, so that you rehearse both reading the diagram and locating the parts on the harness.

Role-play card 1: Reader and locator

Situation

You are standing at a mock harness with the wiring diagram open on the screen.

Your role

You are Technician A, and you read the diagram aloud.

Your goal

Guide your partner to wire W241 by giving the connector, the pin and the full path.

Partner's goal

Your partner locates the parts and calls every reference clearly back to you.

Useful phrases: "It runs from P1 pin 3 …" · "It travels through the harness to J12." · "Say the whole reference back to me."

Role-play card 2: Explaining a suspected open circuit

Situation

The gear-down lamp stays off, and you have just completed a continuity test.

Your role

You are the technician who probed the conductor with the multimeter.

Your goal

Explain clearly why you suspect an open circuit, and describe the next step.

Partner's goal

Your colleague asks how you know it is open rather than a short circuit.

Useful phrases: "The meter reads no continuity." · "Resistance is infinite, so it is an open." · "We re-seat the pins inside the plug."

Role-play card 3: Explaining a suspected short circuit

Situation

A circuit breaker trips, and you suspect a conductor is touching the airframe.

Your role

You inspected the harness and found chafed insulation near a clamp.

Your goal

Explain a suspected short circuit to ground and describe its likely cause.

Partner's goal

Your colleague asks what reading a short circuit gives on the meter.

Useful phrases: "A short reads near zero resistance to ground." · "The insulation is chafed near the clamp." · "The shield may be grounding the core."

Role-play card 4: Shift handover

Situation

Your shift is ending, but the wiring job on the aircraft is not yet finished.

Your role

You hand the open snag over to the next technician on duty.

Your goal

Describe the route you traced and explain clearly what still remains to be done.

Partner's goal

The next technician asks specifically about the splice and the bonding.

Useful phrases: "I traced W241 from P1 pin 3 to J12." · "Please check the splice at TB2." · "The bonding on pin 4 is still open."

07Writing, a tech log corrective-action entry

The task. Write a corrective-action entry for the aircraft tech log. The reported snag is that the gear-down warning lamp does not illuminate during the extension check. Describe how you traced the conductor, what the continuity test showed, and the corrective action you carried out. Write in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English, which means short sentences, one idea in each sentence, and one clear action verb in each step. Aim for roughly seventy to one hundred words.

Model, Corrective action

Traced wire W241 on the wiring diagram. The conductor runs from pin 3 on connector P1 to socket B on connector J12. Did a continuity test with the multimeter. The test showed an open circuit. The resistance read infinite from P1 to J12. Inspected the harness. Found chafed insulation near the clamp. Found no short circuit to ground. Re-seated the pins in the plug. Re-made the splice at terminal block TB2. Confirmed the bonding on pin 4. Re-tested the system. Continuity is now good. The lamp illuminates. Returned the aircraft to service.
