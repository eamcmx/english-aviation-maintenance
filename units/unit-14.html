Assessing an Airworthiness Directive

A new airworthiness directive arrives. Because it is a legal order, the action is mandatory. You must read it, find the required action and the compliance deadline, and brief the planner. This unit gives you the language to do it well.

00The situation

You work at a Part-145 station in Riga. This morning a new airworthiness directive lands in the mailbox, and because it is a legal order, the action is mandatory.

An airworthiness directive is not advice; it is a mandatory order from the authority, so you must comply even when the timing is difficult. Your job now is to assess the directive and brief the planner accurately. First you identify the required corrective action. Then you find the compliance deadline, although the wording is often technical. Finally you agree how and when to comply, because the planning department controls the maintenance slots.

Why does an accurate assessment matter so much? An airworthiness directive corrects an unsafe condition on the aircraft, so a missed deadline is never trivial. If you miss the deadline, non-compliance follows, and non-compliance normally means grounding. A grounded aircraft cannot earn revenue, and the unsafe condition stays open until the corrective action is complete. A clear, disciplined briefing therefore keeps the whole fleet safe and legal.

A good assessment has four short steps, although each demands precision. First, name the directive and its effective date, because the compliance clock starts then. Second, state the required action and say whether it is a one-time task or a recurring inspection. Third, give the compliance deadline in terms the planner can schedule against. Fourth, agree the plan and confirm the next step, so that nothing is left ambiguous.

01Model dialogues

Three short briefings between an engineer and a planner. Each one performs the same job from a different angle: assess the airworthiness directive, weigh the grounding risk, and agree the compliance plan.

Dialogue 1: "A new AD has arrived"

Scene: Karlis, a base engineer, briefs Marta, the planner, on a newly issued directive.

Transcript
A · EngineerHi Marta. A new airworthiness directive arrived this morning, and because it is a legal order, we must plan it now.
B · PlannerThanks, Karlis. Before I open a slot, tell me which aircraft the applicability actually covers.
A · EngineerThe applicability covers our A320 fleet, although only two aircraft appear on the affected list.
B · PlannerGood to know. So what exactly does the airworthiness directive order us to do?
A · EngineerIt orders a recurring inspection of the landing gear pin, and since an AD is mandatory, we have no choice.
B · PlannerI understand. As soon as you can, tell me the effective date, because the deadline depends on it.
A · EngineerThe effective date is the twenty-second of August, and the compliance clock starts on that date.
B · PlannerAnd what is the compliance deadline for the very first inspection?
A · EngineerWe must do the first inspection within five hundred flight cycles, unless the aircraft reaches ninety days first.
B · PlannerThat is soon for the blue aircraft, because it flies almost every single day.
A · EngineerExactly, so we must book it early. Whatever happens, we must not miss the compliance deadline.
B · PlannerUnderstood. In case we are late, what is the real consequence for the aircraft?
A · EngineerNon-compliance means grounding, so the aircraft may not fly until we can show compliance.
B · PlannerThen this is top priority, and I will protect a maintenance slot this month.
A · EngineerThank you. I will forward the airworthiness directive and the applicability list right now.
B · PlannerPerfect. As soon as I have them, I will build the plan and share it with you today.

Dialogue 2: "The repeat check and the final fix"

Scene: the same engineer and planner examine the recurring part and the terminating action.

Transcript
A · EngineerMarta, let us look at the repeat part of the directive, because it is not a one-time job.
B · PlannerRight. So the recurring inspection comes back again and again, at a fixed interval?
A · EngineerYes. As soon as we finish the first check, we must repeat it at a set interval.
B · PlannerWhat is the interval, exactly? I need the precise figure for the maintenance plan.
A · EngineerThe interval is fifteen hundred flight cycles, or twelve months, whichever comes first.
B · PlannerAnd the first threshold, when is the very first inspection actually due?
A · EngineerThe threshold is five hundred flight cycles, counted from the effective date of the directive.
B · PlannerSome airworthiness directives count in flight hours. Does this one use flight hours as well?
A · EngineerNo. This directive counts flight cycles only, since one cycle means one takeoff and landing.
B · PlannerGood. And why did the authority issue this airworthiness directive in the first place?
A · EngineerThey identified an unsafe condition, because the pin can crack under heavy load.
B · PlannerI see. So the recurring inspection is the corrective action, at least for now?
A · EngineerExactly. The inspection is the short-term corrective action, although there is also a terminating action.
B · PlannerTell me about the terminating action, because it sounds important for the long-term plan.
A · EngineerWe fit an improved pin, and once it is installed, we need not repeat the inspection.
B · PlannerSo the terminating action ends the recurring inspection completely. That helps the plan a lot.
A · EngineerIt does, although the terminating action still has its own separate compliance deadline.
B · PlannerThen I will plan both together: the recurring checks and the final terminating fix.

Dialogue 3: "The plan and the grounding risk"

Scene: the engineer and planner agree the compliance plan and the required records.

Transcript
A · EngineerMarta, can we agree the final compliance plan today, because the deadline is getting close?
B · PlannerYes. The planning department has two slots, so tell me which one fits the directive best.
A · EngineerThe first inspection must be done before the fifth of September, without exception.
B · PlannerThe early slot works well, because it falls on the second of September.
A · EngineerGood. That keeps us comfortably inside the compliance deadline.
B · PlannerWhat about the paperwork? Once the check is finished, what exactly must we record?
A · EngineerAfter the check, we must make a logbook entry, since that entry proves compliance.
B · PlannerUnderstood. Does the logbook entry also need the airworthiness directive number?
A · EngineerYes. The logbook entry shall quote the AD number and the date of the action.
B · PlannerNoted. And is this airworthiness directive linked to any older document?
A · EngineerIt replaces an old service bulletin, so that bulletin is now formally superseded.
B · PlannerSo we must not use the old bulletin, provided I have understood you correctly?
A · EngineerCorrect. The directive is the legal order, and it always comes first.
B · PlannerAnd if the planning department cannot find a slot in time?
A · EngineerThen the aircraft is grounded, because non-compliance is simply not an option.
B · PlannerAgreed. I will protect this slot, so that nothing else may take it.
A · EngineerThank you. Please send the finished plan to me and to the technical office.
B · PlannerI will. We stay safe, legal, and on time, which is exactly the goal.

03Grammar in context, must / shall for obligation

An airworthiness directive is a legal order, so the language of obligation is strong. We use must and shall to express that obligation clearly.

Look at these lines from the dialogues, because each one carries an obligation:

  • "We must do the first inspection within five hundred flight cycles." (Dialogue 1)
  • "The first inspection must be done before the fifth of September." (Dialogue 3)
  • "The logbook entry shall quote the AD number and the date." (Dialogue 3)
  • "We must not use the old bulletin." (Dialogue 3)

The pattern is consistent, although the meanings differ. Use must plus the base verb for a strong obligation, as in "we must comply". Use must + be + past participle when the action is passive, as in "must be done", because the aircraft does not act on itself. Use shall in written, legal text, such as a directive or a work order, since it carries a formal, binding tone. Use must not for a prohibition. Importantly, there is never a to after must or shall, and the verb never changes for he, she, or it.

Now watch one pair that learners often confuse, because the difference is safety-critical. Must not means a prohibition: you are not allowed to do it. Need not, or "do not have to", means there is no obligation: you may do it, although nobody requires it. After the terminating action, we need not repeat the inspection, since the duty has ended; that is clearly not a prohibition.

Why does the correct modal matter so much? An airworthiness directive is legally binding, so the modal effectively carries the weight of the regulation. "The AD must be done" tells the planner that compliance is not negotiable, whereas a vague verb can quietly hide the obligation. A hidden obligation is a genuine safety risk, so strong, precise modals keep the fleet safe and the paperwork honest.

UseStructureExample at level
Obligation (active)must + base verbWe must comply before the compliance deadline.
Obligation (passive)must + be + past participleThe airworthiness directive must be done on time.
Legal / written obligationshall + base verbThe operator shall report compliance to the authority.
Prohibition (a ban)must not + base verbYou must not fly the aircraft after the deadline.

Three common mistakes (Latvian / Russian speakers)

✗ The airworthiness directive must to be done by Friday.
✓ The airworthiness directive must be done by Friday. After must and shall, use the base verb only. Do not add to, because Latvian and Russian speakers often insert it here.
✗ The inspection must done before the compliance deadline.
✓ The inspection must be done before the compliance deadline. A passive obligation always needs be, because the pattern is must + be + past participle.
✗ The check is no longer required, so we must not repeat it.
✓ The check is no longer required, so we need not repeat it. must not is a prohibition, whereas need not or "do not have to" means there is no obligation.

Some pivot pins can crack under load. A crack can lead to failure of the landing gear. Applicability: Model A320 aircraft, serial numbers 5100 through 5140. Required action (corrective action): Do a detailed inspection of the pivot pin. Compliance threshold: Within 500 flight cycles after the effective date, or within 90 days, whichever comes first. More useful phrases

Keep these ready for any AD briefing. Say them out loud twice.

  • Open: "A new airworthiness directive arrived this morning, so we must plan it now."
  • State the action: "It orders a recurring inspection, and the action is mandatory."
  • Give the date: "The effective date is the twenty-second of August, so the clock starts then."
  • Give the deadline: "The first check must be done within five hundred flight cycles."
  • Warn clearly: "We must not miss the compliance deadline, whatever happens."
  • Name the risk: "Non-compliance will ground the aircraft until we can show compliance."
  • Confirm records: "The logbook entry shall quote the airworthiness directive number."
  • Close: "We stay safe, legal, and on time, which is exactly the goal."

07Key points to remember

A short review before you finish. Read each line and check that you can do it.

  • An airworthiness directive is a legal order, so the required action is always mandatory.
  • Check the applicability first, because it tells you whether your aircraft is affected.
  • Find the effective date, since the compliance clock for the deadline starts on that date.
  • Read the required corrective action and the compliance deadline before you plan any work.
  • Decide whether the check is a one-time task or a recurring inspection that repeats.
  • A recurring inspection repeats at a set interval, counted in flight cycles or flight hours.
  • A terminating action can end the recurring inspection for good, although it has its own deadline.
  • Non-compliance normally means grounding, so we must not miss the deadline.
  • Use must and shall to express obligation, and remember there is never a "to" after them.
  • Record the completed work with a logbook entry that quotes the airworthiness directive number.

These disciplined habits keep the aircraft safe and the paperwork clean, although they take a little time. They also build trust with the planning department, because a clear briefing is easy to schedule. An engineer who assesses an AD accurately gets the work booked on time, which is the goal of this whole unit.
