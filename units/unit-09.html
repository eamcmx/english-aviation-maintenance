Talking through a troubleshooting approach

Two technicians look at the same fault and reason out loud, because guessing wastes time and good parts. Is the problem electrical or mechanical? They propose a test sequence, use conditional language to link each test to a likely result, and finally agree what to try next.

00The scenario

You are in the hangar with a colleague, although neither of you knows yet why the right flap moves so slowly. One of you suspects the fault is electrical, while the other is convinced it is mechanical. You cannot simply change parts at random, because that approach wastes both time and serviceable units. Instead, you must reason it out together, propose a sensible test, and agree the next step before you touch anything.

Good troubleshooting is really a disciplined habit of thought, rather than luck or intuition. You always start from the visible symptom, and then you work backwards towards the underlying root cause. Before each test, you say clearly what result you expect. If a test passes, you have eliminated one possible cause; if it fails, you have learned something valuable instead. Throughout this unit you rehearse that out-loud reasoning with a colleague, and you use the first conditional to connect every test to its likely outcome.

03Grammar in context — the first conditional

In troubleshooting you constantly link a likely test to a likely result, and English does this neatly with the first conditional. You state what you will do, and you state what happens if the condition turns out to be true. Look carefully at these three lines taken directly from the dialogues.

“If the motor stays silent, the fault is probably electrical.” — Dialogue 1
“If it fails the bench test, we replace the actuator.” — Dialogue 1
“If a wire fails the bench test, we build a new harness.” — Dialogue 2

The rule itself is short, although many learners still apply it incorrectly. In the if-clause you must use the present tense, and never will. The other clause carries the result, and it can take will, the present tense, or a direct order. So you correctly say “if it fails, we replace it”, and never “if it will fail”. Importantly, the same rule also holds after when, as soon as and unless.

FunctionStructureExample
Likely test → likely resultIf + present, + presentIf the breaker trips, the light goes off.
Test → instructionIf + present, + orderIf it fails the test, replace it.
Cause → future effectIf + present, + willIf the harness moves, the reading will jump.
Negative conditionIf + present negative, + resultIf there is no continuity, the wire is broken.

Three common mistakes (Latvian and Russian speakers)

✗ If it will fail the bench test, we replace it.
✓ If it fails the bench test, we replace it.
Why: use the present tense in the if-clause, never will, although Latvian and Russian often use a future form here.
✗ If breaker trips, technician resets it.
✓ If the breaker trips, the technician resets it.
Why: English needs the article the, whereas Latvian and Russian have no articles, so learners frequently drop them.
✗ When you will find a short circuit, tell me.
✓ As soon as you find a short circuit, tell me.
Why: after when, if or as soon as, use the present tense, because a future form is not allowed in the time clause.

Read an AMM fault-isolation task

Read the task. Then answer the six questions. Write short answers.

AIRCRAFT MAINTENANCE MANUAL — TASK 27-51-00 — RIGHT FLAP SLOW

The purpose of this task is to isolate the fault when the right flap moves slowly. The symptom is simple: the flap still moves, but it is noticeably slow on one side only.

First, check the circuit breaker on panel P6, and if it is tripped, reset it once and observe it. If the breaker trips again immediately, stop the test, because the root cause may be a short circuit.

Next, open the wiring diagram for the flap motor, and carefully trace the line down to the actuator. Then inspect the connector at the actuator, because a loose connector usually gives an intermittent fault.

After that, run a continuity check on the wire, and if there is no continuity, the wire is broken somewhere. If the wiring is good, remove the actuator and send it away for a bench test.

If the actuator fails the bench test, replace it with a fully serviceable unit. Finally, record every step in the tech log, and sign the release only when the flap is properly fixed.

Caution: do not defer this fault without first consulting the MEL.

  2. Which breaker do you check first?
  3. What may make the breaker trip again?
  4. What do you inspect at the actuator?
  5. What does no continuity mean?
  6. Where do you record each step?

This maintenance readout covers the number one VHF radio on registration YL-ABC. The stored fault code is 23-12, which clearly points to the antenna feeder line.

05Speaking: reason it out with a colleague

Work in pairs, so that one person is technician A and the other is technician B. Use the useful phrases, propose a test, and say what you expect with if. Then agree the next step together, and change roles after each card.

Role-play card 1 — Electrical or mechanical?

Situation The right flap moves slowly, although the cause is not clear yet.
Your role Technician A. You suspect the fault is electrical.
Your goal Propose a logical sequence: check the circuit breaker, then the connector, then a continuity check. Say clearly what each result would mean.
Useful phrases

06Writing: a fault-isolation tech log entry

Write a short tech log entry for the flap fault, and use Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100), because short sentences and approved words are much easier to read on the line. Write one instruction per sentence, keep every sentence under twenty words, and use the present tense in every if-clause, with no will.

Your task

Write 80–110 words to record the troubleshooting. Give the symptom, the tests you ran, and the root cause. Use the first conditional for the next step, so you say clearly what you do if the bench test fails. Keep every sentence short and clear.

Show model answer

TECH LOG ENTRY — RIGHT FLAP SLOW — FAULT ISOLATION

Symptom: the right flap moves slowly on one side, although the left flap is normal.

Test 1: check the circuit breaker on panel P6. The breaker is not tripped.

Test 2: inspect the connector at the actuator. The connector is clean and tight.

Test 3: run a continuity check on the wire. The continuity reading is good.

Result: the wiring is not the root cause, so the actuator is probably weak.

Next step: send the actuator for a bench test. If it fails, replace it with a serviceable unit.

Note: the fault is still open in the tech log. Do not defer without the MEL.
