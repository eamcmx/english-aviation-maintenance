Mentoring: explaining a task to a junior.

Coach a new technician through a wheel change. Teach the steps in order, check that the junior has understood, correct a mistake kindly, and link every step to its safety reason.

Unit30, Mentoring a junior Vocabularywalk-through · competence · sign-off authority GrammarSequencers + imperatives Class length90 minutes · self-study

By the end of this unit you can …

  • Can explain a procedure step by step at a level a junior understands.
  • Can check understanding and correct errors constructively.
  • Can relate the task to its safety and airworthiness rationale.

01The situation

You are an experienced technician in a busy Part-145 hangar. Today a junior joins your team for a main-wheel change. You must genuinely teach the task, not simply complete it yourself. Therefore you demonstrate each separate step in the correct order. You continually check that the junior follows, and you correct any error immediately. You also explain clearly why each individual step keeps the aircraft airworthy.

Effective mentoring is essentially ordered teaching. Because a wheel change follows a fixed sequence, you deliberately teach it as ordered steps. You give one clear instruction at a time.

Listen carefully to the three dialogues first, before you read the actual words. Try to catch the individual steps, and the one deliberate error. Then open the transcript and read along attentively. After that, build the specialist vocabulary, study the grammar, and complete the eight exercises. Finish with the speaking cards and the writing task, unless your teacher directs you differently. Throughout, notice how the mentor deliberately gives one short instruction at a time, because an inexperienced junior cannot possibly follow ten separate steps at once.

02Model dialogues

Listen first with the transcript closed. Then open it and read along.

Dialogue 1: The walk-through

A = Karlis, mentor (experienced B1)  ·  B = Dmitrijs, junior technician

Transcript

KARLISDmitrijs, today I will mentor you through a complete main-wheel change, so watch my walk-through carefully first.

DMITRIJSThank you. Although I have observed a wheel change before, this is my first without close supervision.

KARLISGood. Because this is on-the-job training, you learn the procedure properly while we complete real work.

DMITRIJSWhere exactly do we start? Should I read the task card before I touch anything?

KARLISYes. First, read the task card completely, because it lists every step in the correct order.

DMITRIJSI have it here. Step one instructs me to lift the aircraft carefully on the jack.

KARLISCorrect. However, before you jack it, always check the surrounding area for foreign object debris.

DMITRIJSUnderstood. The area is completely clear, so now I can raise the aircraft on the jack.

KARLISGood. Let me give you a short demonstration of the next step, so you copy it exactly.

DMITRIJSPlease do. I generally learn faster when I watch carefully, and then get hands-on practice.

KARLISWatch the axle closely. First, remove the old wheel; then inspect the axle for cracks.

DMITRIJSWhy must we inspect the axle every single time? What exactly is the rationale behind it?

KARLISThe rationale is airworthiness, because a cracked axle can suddenly fail while the aircraft is in service.

DMITRIJSThat makes complete sense. So the underlying safety reason really drives each separate step.

KARLISExactly right. Now, a quick comprehension check: which important action comes before jacking the aircraft?

DMITRIJSThe foreign object debris check, and reading the entire task card in the correct order.

Dialogue 2: Torque, lockwire, and a deliberate error

A = Karlis, mentor  ·  B = Dmitrijs, junior technician

Transcript

KARLISThe new wheel is now on the axle. Fit the nut carefully, and then torque it correctly.

DMITRIJSI will tighten it immediately. I am using this ordinary ratchet from the tool trolley.

KARLISStop for a second, please. A ratchet is completely wrong here, so which tool do we use?

DMITRIJSSorry, the torque wrench, obviously. It tightens the nut accurately to an exact, specified value.

KARLISGood catch. Never guess the torque; instead, always set the torque wrench correctly first.

DMITRIJSUnderstood completely. The task card specifies three hundred newton metres for this particular wheel nut.

KARLISCorrect. First, set the exact value; then torque the nut steadily, using smooth, controlled pulls.

DMITRIJSDone. The wrench clicked precisely at the set value, so the applied torque is correct.

KARLISNow the critical safety step. After torque, what actually stops the nut from turning loose?

DMITRIJSThe lockwire, definitely. I fit the lockwire carefully through the nut and around the hub.

KARLISShow me, please. Unfortunately, your lockwire is currently running in the wrong direction.

DMITRIJSIs it really? Honestly, I assumed that either direction would be perfectly acceptable.

KARLISNo. The lockwire must always pull the nut tight, never loose, so let me explain properly.

DMITRIJSI clearly see it now. Thank you for the constructive feedback, not simply the word “wrong”.

KARLISThat is precisely how I mentor. I show the actual fault, and I always give a reason.

DMITRIJSHonest feedback genuinely helps me build competence, so I will definitely not repeat that mistake.

Dialogue 3: Checking understanding and sign-off authority

A = Karlis, mentor  ·  B = Dmitrijs, junior technician

Transcript

KARLISThe wheel change is completely finished. Let me carefully check your understanding one more time.

DMITRIJSPlease do. I genuinely want to be certain that I can perform this task alone.

KARLISFirst question: why exactly do we torque the nut before we fit the lockwire?

DMITRIJSBecause the lockwire only secures a nut that has already been torqued correctly.

KARLISGood answer. Next question: what is the fundamental rationale for the entire job?

DMITRIJSAirworthiness, clearly. A loose main wheel represents a serious and unacceptable safety risk.

KARLISCorrect. Your competence is steadily growing. However, can you actually sign this job off?

DMITRIJSNot yet, unfortunately. I do not have sign-off authority for a wheel change.

KARLISRight. Sign-off authority requires a valid licence and a formal competence assessment.

DMITRIJSSo today genuinely counts as on-the-job training toward that important assessment?

KARLISYes, exactly. I carefully record your progress, and I always give you honest feedback.

DMITRIJSSo what is your honest feedback on today's complete wheel change?

KARLISStrong, ordered work, although I had to correct the lockwire direction once.

DMITRIJSThank you sincerely. When can I finally work confidently without your direct supervision?

KARLISOnly after more supervised practice, and after you pass the formal competence assessment.

DMITRIJSUnderstood completely. Until then, you keep the sign-off authority, and I keep learning steadily.

04Grammar in context, sequencers and imperatives

Teaching a task is essentially ordered instruction. Therefore we use sequencers to signal the order, and imperatives to deliver each individual step.

Look carefully at these lines from the dialogues. The mentor names the order with a sequencer, and then delivers a short command. There is deliberately no subject before the verb. Instead, the imperative verb itself begins the instruction.

From Dialogue 1: “First, read the task card.”, “First, remove the old wheel; then inspect the axle.”
From Dialogue 2: “First, set the value; then torque the nut.”, “Always set the torque wrench first.”
From Dialogue 3: “First question: why do we torque before the lockwire?”

Notice exactly why this structure suits teaching so well. An inexperienced junior cannot possibly hold ten separate steps at once. Therefore the mentor deliberately breaks the whole job into single steps. Each sequencer marks the following step, although the underlying order stays completely fixed. Because the imperative is short, the junior immediately hears one clear action, and can follow it correctly.

The form itself is genuinely simple. Start with a sequencer, then a comma, and then the imperative verb. The imperative is the plain verb, deliberately with no subject in front. Use exactly this structure for every teaching step.

SequencerImperative verbRest of the stepFull instruction
First,readthe task cardFirst, read the task card.
Next,setthe torque wrenchNext, set the torque wrench.
Then,torquethe nutThen, torque the nut.
Finally,fitthe lockwireFinally, fit the lockwire.

Common sequencers for teaching: First, Next, Then, After that, Once …, As soon as …, Finally. Use one per step, so the order is always clear.

Three common mistakes (Latvian & Russian speakers)

✗ In first, remove the old wheel. ✓ First, remove the old wheel. Do not say in first. That copies the Russian and Latvian phrase. In English the sequencer is just First.
✗ First, put wheel on axle. ✓ First, put the wheel on the axle. Do not drop the. Latvian and Russian have no articles, so it is easy to lose. English needs the before the known part.
✗ Then you are torquing the nut. ✓ Then, torque the nut. For a teaching step, use the plain imperative. Do not use the continuous or add you. Just start with the verb.

07Writing task

Write a short set of teaching steps for a junior, in Simplified Technical English.

A teaching instruction must be clear and ordered. So each step starts with a sequencer, then a plain imperative. Because a junior follows your words exactly, imperfect wording is unsafe. Keep one action per step, and give the safety reason where it matters.

Task. A junior must learn the first six steps of a main-wheel change. Write the six steps as teaching instructions (about 60–90 words). Use a sequencer and an imperative in each step. Add the safety rationale for at least one step. Keep sentences short.

Simplified Technical English: write it clean

One idea per sentence. Use short, common words. Use the same word for the same thing. Keep each sentence under 20 words. Use the imperative: read the card, not you should read the card.

Show a model answer
Model answer First, read the task card. Next, check the area for foreign object debris, because loose items can damage the aircraft. Then, lift the aircraft on the jack. After that, remove the old wheel. Now, inspect the axle for cracks; a cracked axle can fail in service. Finally, fit the new wheel, and set the torque wrench before you torque the nut.
