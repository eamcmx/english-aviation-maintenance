Giving a shift handover briefing.

The night shift is walking in. Three aircraft are on your board: one finished, one with a deferred defect, one still mid-task. This unit gives you the language to hand it all over clearly, so nothing is lost between shifts.

01The situation

It is 18:00 at a Part-145 line station. Your day shift is ending. Three aircraft are on your board. The A320 is finished and ready to fly. The 737 carries a deferred defect on the MEL. The ATR is mid-task, with a radar fault still open. The night technician arrives for the handover. You must brief them clearly, so nothing is missed.

A handover is a short but high-value conversation, because the incoming technician depends entirely on the information you pass across. You summarise what you have finished, what you have started, and what is still outstanding for the next shift. You highlight every safety-critical item first, since a missed defect could delay a departure or even endanger an aircraft. After that, you answer the incoming technician's questions about status as accurately as you possibly can. Listen to three realistic briefings below, and then gradually build the same professional language yourself.

04Grammar in context: present perfect vs past simple

In a handover, you mix two tenses. You use the present perfect for work that is done and still matters now. You use the past simple for a finished detail with a clear past time. Look at these lines from the dialogues.

"I have completed the left engine oil change." — done, and it matters now (the engine is ready).
"I ran the leak check at 15:00." — a finished action with a clear past time.
"Have you found the cause yet?" — up to now; the word yet needs the present perfect.

The rule is simple. If the time is finished (at 15:00, yesterday, this morning at 08:00), use the past simple. If you look at the result now, or use already, yet, just, so far, use the present perfect.

In practice, technicians move between the two tenses within a single briefing, because each tense answers a different question for the listener. The present perfect answers "what is the situation right now?", whereas the past simple answers "what exactly happened, and when did it happen?". If you say "I have completed the inspection", your colleague immediately understands that the aircraft is ready at this moment. However, as soon as you add a finished time, such as "at fourteen hundred", English forces you to switch back to the past simple. Getting this contrast right is exactly what makes a handover sound accurate and professional, rather than vague or confusing.

UseFormSignal wordsExample
Done and relevant nowhave / has + past participlealready, just, so farI have signed the task card.
Not done up to nowhave / has not + past participleyet, still notWe have not found the cause yet.
Finished past detailpast simple verbat 15:00, yesterday, thenI ran the leak check at 15:00.
Question about statusHave / Has + subject + participleyet, alreadyHave you inspected the connector yet?

Three common mistakes (Latvian and Russian speakers)

✗ I already signed off the task card.
✓ I have already signed off the task card.
Latvian and Russian have no present perfect, so speakers use the past simple with already. In English, already needs the present perfect.
✗ I have closed the defect at 14:00.
✓ I closed the defect at 14:00.
A clear past time (14:00) needs the past simple. Speakers over-use the perfect because their first language marks the result, not the time.
✗ Did you finish the borescope yet?
✓ Have you finished the borescope yet?
The word yet asks about now, so it needs the present perfect. Russian and Latvian often drop the auxiliary have.

09The paperwork you will meet

A handover is only as good as the paperwork behind it. Every fact you pass on comes from a document, and you must read each one quickly and correctly. This section shows the main genres you will handle on the line and in the hangar. Learn what each one is for, because the incoming technician trusts the paperwork, not your memory.

  • Tech log, the aircraft's running record of defects and work.
  • Task card, one job broken into numbered steps.
  • Work order, an authorised instruction to do a job.
  • AMM, the approved Aircraft Maintenance Manual.
  • IPC, the Illustrated Parts Catalogue, for the right part number.
  • MEL, the list of what may be inoperative, and for how long.
  • SB, a Service Bulletin, often recommended.
  • AD, an Airworthiness Directive, always mandatory.
  • BITE readout, the result of a built-in self-test.
  • Wiring diagram, the map of an electrical circuit.

10Build a clear handover

A clear handover has three parts for each aircraft: what is done, what is in progress, and what is outstanding. You also flag every open defect and every safety-critical item. Keep each sentence short. Move from aircraft one to aircraft two to aircraft three in the same order every time, so the incoming technician always knows where you are.

Watch your collocations too. You raise a work order, you apply the MEL, you fit a placard, you run a BITE test, and you release the aircraft to service. The wrong partner word sounds strange and can even change the meaning, so learn the pairs as whole chunks.
