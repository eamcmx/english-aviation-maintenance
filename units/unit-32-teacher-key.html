2 · First-language interference (Latvian & Russian)

These learners share two features that drive most of the errors in this unit: neither language uses articles, and both mark aspect differently from English. Knowing why the error happens lets you recast in one line without a grammar lecture.

Trap 1 — the dropped article

Aircraft is ready for service. → The aircraft is ready for service.

Why: Latvian and Russian have no definite or indefinite article, so learners simply leave it out — it feels complete without one. In a release statement this reads as unfinished and, on a legal record, unclear. Recast: say the correct line once, stress the, and move on. Point to the anchor line, which they already know.

Trap 2 — the wrong preposition

ready to service → ready for service

Why: both first languages map the idea with a dative / direction sense (“to”), and English collocations rarely transfer one-to-one. Ready for is fixed. Recast: treat it as a chunk to memorise, not a rule to reason out; drill ready for service as one word-block.

Trap 3 — the continuous for a status

The pump is being unserviceable. → The pump is unserviceable.

Why: Russian and Latvian express states and processes through verb aspect, and learners over-use the English continuous when they want to sound “present”. But a status is a fact now, so English uses the present simple. Recast: contrast a status (is unserviceable) with an action in progress (is changing the pump) so the difference is felt, not just told.

Trap 4 — the passive without the past participle

The test was carry out. → The test was carried out.

Why: learners build the passive from the base form because their first language forms it differently (often with a reflexive or a change of ending, not be + participle). The -ed gets lost, especially in speech. Recast: highlight be + past participle as the frame; repeat was carried out, has been done, is signed as a set.

Extra sound trap (both L1s): the number pairs thirty / thirteen and forty / fourteen. Stress falls differently (THIR-ty vs thir-TEEN). Drill the stress, and teach the safe check: “Do you mean one-three, or three-zero?”

3 · Extension activities

  • Full simulation run (20 min). In fours, run the whole event end to end: one shift lead, one mechanic, one avionics engineer, one certifying staff. They must reach the signed release and finish on the anchor line. Others watch and note one strong sentence and one error.
  • Defect of the day (10 min). Hand each pair a new one-line finding (for example a cracked lens, a loose bracket, a failed BITE self-test). They decide: serviceable or unserviceable, defer under the MEL or rectify now, and whether a duplicate inspection is needed. They report in three sentences.
  • Tech-log rewrite (10 min). Give a wordy, weak tech-log paragraph. Pairs rewrite it in Simplified Technical English — short sentences, correct tense, no dropped articles. Compare versions.
  • Number dictation (5 min). Read ten figures and times at speed (forty, thirty-nine, three days, ten days, eighteen hundred, oh six thirty, thirty-two degrees…). Learners write digits. Swap and check.
  • Mixed AD/SB/MEL sort (5 min). Twelve short statements; learners sort into airworthiness directive, service bulletin, or minimum equipment list, and justify with one word (mandatory / recommended / dispatch).
  • AI partner (self-study). The worksheet's conversation partner runs the three scenarios one-to-one at B2 and recasts errors gently — good homework before the quiz.
