01The situation

You work at a busy line station. Most of your colleagues speak English as a second language, so English is the shared lingua franca. Today a part number on a task card does not match the wiring diagram. You call the OEM support desk, and the engineer there is also a non-native speaker. Two codes sound almost the same, and a small misunderstanding could send the wrong pin into service.

When nobody is a native speaker, you cannot rely on perfect grammar. Instead, you rely on strategies. You paraphrase, you confirm, and you check back before you act.

Listen to the three dialogues first, before you read the words. Try to catch the misunderstanding, and how the speakers fix it. Then open the transcript and read along. After that, build the vocabulary, study the question tags, and do the eight exercises. Finish with the speaking cards and the writing task, unless your teacher tells you otherwise. Throughout, notice one habit: the speakers never assume they understood. They say the reference back, and they wait for a clear yes.

Dialogue 1: The connector reference

A = Rihards, line technician (Latvia)  ·  B = Marco, OEM support engineer (Italy)

Transcript

RIHARDSHello Marco, this is Rihards at the line station. You currently support the OEM desk, don't you?

MARCOYes, I do. So how exactly can I help you today with your connector problem?

RIHARDSUnfortunately we have a misunderstanding about one connector reference. Could you possibly clarify it?

MARCOCertainly. Give me the exact part number, and then we examine the wiring diagram together.

RIHARDSThe reference printed on the task card is P12. Presumably that is a single pin, isn't it?

MARCONo, not exactly. P12 identifies the connector, although TP12 is actually the test pin. That is the misunderstanding.

RIHARDSAh, understood. So allow me to paraphrase it, to confirm I interpreted you correctly.

MARCOPlease do. Honestly, it is considerably safer if you repeat it back in your own words.

RIHARDSP12 is the complete connector. TP12 is one individual test pin inside it. Correct?

MARCOExactly correct. You paraphrased it perfectly, so evidently we now share no misunderstanding.

RIHARDSGood. The two letters sound remarkably similar on the phone, so could you spell out the second reference?

MARCOCertainly. Using the phonetic alphabet: Tango, Papa, one, two. Together that spells TP12.

RIHARDSLet me read back carefully: Tango, Papa, one, two. That now matches my wiring diagram precisely.

MARCOPerfect. Originally the reference was ambiguous, because the two codes look almost identical.

RIHARDSThank you. Plain words and a quick check back genuinely prevented a serious error here.

MARCOYou are welcome. Always confirm a connector reference directly with the OEM before you fit it.

Dialogue 2: Hangar talk and a BITE readout

A = Rihards, line technician  ·  B = Anna, avionics technician

Transcript

RIHARDSAnna, just a quick question. The BITE readout returned a code I cannot read, could you help?

ANNACertainly. Show me the readout on the panel. What exactly does the message actually say?

RIHARDSIt clearly says “TP12 open”. Presumably that indicates the test pin, isn't it?

ANNAYes, the test pin. Unfortunately the readout uses very short codes, so they become ambiguous quickly.

RIHARDSAdmittedly this is only hangar talk, so we can relax the formal register slightly, can't we?

ANNAWe can, although we still confirm the code carefully. Repeat it back, so I am completely certain.

RIHARDSOkay. “TP12 open” evidently means the test pin currently has no connection. That is it, right?

ANNAPrecisely. Now allow me to rephrase the correction in plain, ordinary words for you.

RIHARDSPlease do. English is our shared lingua franca, so genuinely simple words help everybody.

ANNARight. Reseat the connector, then run the BITE test again. Reasonably straightforward, yeah?

RIHARDSUnderstood completely. Reseat the connector and re-run the BITE test. Did I clarify that correctly?

ANNAAbsolutely. However, if the readout still displays open, we immediately contact the OEM desk.

RIHARDSAnd in the tech log I obviously record it formally, not in this casual hangar register, right?

ANNACorrect. Conversation stays casual, although the written record remains formal, accurate and clear.

RIHARDSThat makes sense. Casual conversation, formal documentation, so I deliberately switch between two registers.

Dialogue 3: Confirming the fix with the lead

A = Rihards, line technician  ·  B = Valdis, shift lead

Transcript

RIHARDSValdis, I urgently need to report a connector issue and confirm the correction with you.

VALDISGo ahead. Present it to me formally first, and afterwards we discuss it together.

RIHARDSThe task card reference P12 was unfortunately misread as the test pin TP12.

VALDISSo evidently there was a misunderstanding over the reference. You resolved it with the OEM, didn't you?

RIHARDSYes. I telephoned the OEM desk and requested that they clarify the two similar codes.

VALDISGood. Now paraphrase precisely what they explained, so that I can confirm we genuinely agree.

RIHARDSP12 identifies the connector; TP12 is one individual test pin inside it. That is confirmed.

VALDISCorrect. And you verified the wiring diagram against the exact part number, didn't you?

RIHARDSI did. I additionally read back each code in the phonetic alphabet, to prevent any mistake.

VALDISThat is precisely the check back we require on a safety-critical reference.

RIHARDSNow, informally: the two codes appeared almost identical, so honestly it was an easy slip.

VALDISI understand. On the shop floor we can be casual, although the tech log always stays formal.

RIHARDSUnderstood. I will document it in plain, formal English for the permanent record.

VALDISGood. Confirm the connector is correctly fitted and the BITE test passes, won't you?

RIHARDSI will. I'll re-run the test, confirm the readout is clear, and formally sign it back to you.

04Grammar in context, question tags for confirmation

In a shared second language, you check meaning all the time. A short question tag turns a statement into a quick check. “That's the correct pin, right?”

Look at these lines from the dialogues. The speaker states something, and then adds a small tag. The tag asks the other person to confirm. It is fast, and it keeps the talk safe.

From Dialogue 1: “You support the OEM desk, don't you?”, “That is a single pin, isn't it?”, “TP12 is one test pin inside it. Right?”
From Dialogue 2: “We can drop the formal register a bit, can't we?”, “That's it, right?”
From Dialogue 3: “You cleared it with the OEM, didn't you?”, “…the BITE test passes, won't you?”

Here is the rule. If the statement is positive, the tag is negative. If the statement is negative, the tag is positive. The tag uses the same helping verb and tense as the statement. For a statement with no helping verb, use do, does, or did. In easy talk, you can also use right? for any statement.

Notice why this suits a lingua franca so well. You do not need perfect grammar to be safe. You state the fact, you add a tag, and you wait for a clear yes. If the answer is no, you found the misunderstanding early, before you fit the wrong part.

StatementPolarityTagFull confirmation question
It is the correct pin,positiveisn't it?It is the correct pin, isn't it?
You sent the report,positivedidn't you?You sent the report, didn't you?

Common tags for confirming: isn't it?, doesn't it?, didn't you?, won't you?, can't we?, and the easy right?. Use one, and then wait for the answer.

Three common mistakes (Latvian & Russian speakers)

It is the correct pin, no? It is the correct pin, isn't it? Do not use one frozen tag like no? Russian and Latvian use an invariant tag (net?, vai ne?). English changes the helping verb each time.
You sent the report, isn't it? You sent the report, didn't you? Do not use isn't it? for every sentence. The tag must match the verb and the tense. Here the verb is sent, so the tag is didn't you?
That is not the right connector, isn't it? That is not the right connector, is it? A negative statement takes a positive tag. Flip the polarity: negative fact, positive tag.

07Writing task

Write a short tech-log entry that records how you cleared a misunderstanding, in Simplified Technical English.

A tech-log entry must be clear and formal. So you write short sentences, one idea each. Because the record is permanent, plain words matter more than clever ones. Report the fault, the clarification, and the fix, in that order.

Task. A BITE readout showed “TP12 open”. You called the OEM and cleared a reference misunderstanding, then reseated the connector. Write the tech-log entry (about 60–90 words). Report the fault, how you confirmed the meaning, and the fix. Keep the register formal.

Simplified Technical English: write it clean

One idea per sentence. Use short, common words. Use the same word for the same thing. Keep each sentence under 20 words. Use the imperative or the plain past: reseated the connector, not the connector was being reseated by me.
