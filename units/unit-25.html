Reporting a near-miss or hangar incident

A dropped tool. No one hurt, this time. In this unit you report the event to the safety officer with a clear, factual timeline, and you suggest one way to stop it happening again.

SpecialismAircraft mechanics & avionics (EASA Part-66/145) Grammar focusPast continuous for background action Vocabulary fieldSafety reporting: near-miss, root cause, action Class length90 minutes · self-study ready

By the end of this unit you can …

  • Can report an incident factually, separating observation from opinion.
  • Can describe the sequence of events using clear past-tense narration.
  • Can suggest corrective actions without assigning blame.

01The situation

You are a Part-145 mechanic on the late shift. You are torquing panel bolts on a flap, up on a work platform. Your torque wrench slips and falls two metres to the hangar floor. Nobody is hurt, and the aircraft is not damaged, but it was close. This is a near-miss (an event that almost caused harm). You must report it to the safety officer now, while the facts are fresh. You will give a plain timeline, keep facts apart from opinion, and suggest one action to prevent the next one.

Reporting openly is not about blame. A good report helps the team find the root cause (the first, real reason) and agree a corrective action (a step to stop it happening again). Listen to the three dialogues first, then read the transcripts. After that, work through the vocabulary, the grammar, and the tasks.

In professional aviation maintenance, the quality of a safety report depends on discipline, and not on drama. A useful occurrence report records precisely what happened, in the correct order, using neutral language that any investigator can trust. Whenever you describe the event, you must separate your observations, which are verifiable facts, from your interpretation, which is only opinion. This habit protects your colleagues, because an honest, blame-free report allows the organisation to identify the underlying weakness and correct it before another technician is exposed to exactly the same hazard.

02Model dialogues

Listen first, with the transcript closed, because a safety report is spoken before it is written. Then open the transcript and read along carefully. Throughout, A is the mechanic reporting the event, while B is the safety officer or a colleague.

Dialogue 1

Reporting the near-miss at once

Immediately after the tool falls, the mechanic hurries over to find the safety officer in Bay 3.

Transcript

ARita, I have to report a near-miss immediately, because a tool just fell from my platform.

BI am the safety officer, so tell me everything, but first, is anyone actually injured?

ANobody is injured, although it was extremely close, so I stopped the whole task at once.

BThat was exactly the right decision. Now, give me an observation, and not an opinion.

AUnderstood. I was torquing the flap panel bolts when the torque wrench slipped out of my hand.

BSo you were working directly on the flap. Where exactly were you standing at that moment?

AI was standing on the elevated platform, near the trailing edge, on the right-hand side.

BDescribe the sequence of events for me now, in the exact order that everything happened.

AAs the wrench slipped, it dropped roughly two metres onto the concrete hangar floor below.

BDid the wrench strike anybody, or any part of the aircraft structure, as it fell?

ANo. It landed clear of the aircraft, and fortunately nobody was walking underneath at the time.

BIt is still a serious hazard, because a dropped tool is a genuine incident, even without damage.

AI completely agree, which is exactly why I am reporting it now, before we forget the details.

BCorrect. It also counts as FOD, since a loose tool can easily damage the aircraft structure.

AExactly. I have already placed a barrier around the area, so that nobody accidentally steps on it.

BPerfect response. I will log the occurrence, and we will hold a proper debrief in ten minutes.

Dialogue 2

The debrief: finding the root cause

Ten minutes later, the two of them sit down together to investigate why the tool actually fell.

Transcript

ARita, I have already written down the full sequence of events for the occurrence report.

BGood work. Let us find the root cause together, and not simply blame the last mistake.

AThe wrench slipped because my hand was tired, and because the bolt was slightly over-torqued.

BThat is certainly one cause. However, let us also look carefully for any contributing factors.

AWell, the lighting was poor, since I was working in a deep shadow underneath the flap.

BPoor lighting is a clear contributing factor. Now, what exactly did the work order say?

AThe work order did not mention a lanyard, so unfortunately I was not using one.

BThat is important, because a missing instruction is a genuine cause, and never just an excuse.

AI also checked the tech log, and the same access stand had a loose rail last week.

BSo the stand itself may be a hazard, which means we should inspect it thoroughly today.

AAgreed. The root cause really looks like weak tool control, rather than just my tired grip.

BI think so too, since no lanyard, poor light, and a shaky stand together caused this.

AShould I add all of these details to the report as separate, clearly numbered observations?

BYes. Keep every line factual, one observation per line, in order, and with absolutely no blame.

AUnderstood. I will keep the objective facts and my personal opinion very clearly apart.

Dialogue 3

Agreeing corrective and preventive actions

After the debrief has finished, the mechanic explains the agreed plan to a colleague named Marek.

Transcript

AMarek, we have finally finished the debrief on my dropped-tool near-miss from the late shift.

BI heard about it earlier. Nobody was injured, although honestly it could have been very bad.

BSo tell me, what corrective action did the investigation team eventually agree on?

AFirst, every tool used above floor level must have a lanyard attached, from now on.

BThat makes complete sense, because a lanyard physically stops the tool falling to the floor.

ASecond, we will install much better lighting underneath the flap, as a practical mitigation.

BGood, since poor light was a contributing factor, so brighter lighting clearly lowers the risk.

AThird, we will repair the loose access stand before anyone is allowed to use it again.

BAnd what preventive action have you planned for the future, before the very next job?

AYes, we will update the work order, so that it requires a documented lanyard check.

BExcellent. That way, tool control begins before the task starts, instead of afterwards.

ARita also wants a short toolbox talk about dropped tools, sometime later this week.

BVery smart, because if we share the lesson properly, then the whole team learns from it.

AExactly. The real point is to fix the broken system, and never to blame a single person.

BFully agreed. As long as we keep reporting openly, we can prevent the next serious incident.

Audio 4 · Monologue

Hangar safety announcement

A short stand-down announcement over the hangar speakers. You answer questions on it in E8's partner track, but listen now for the main message.

Transcript

PAAttention, all hangar staff. This is the safety officer speaking to you directly.

PAEarlier today we had a dropped-tool near-miss in Bay 3, although fortunately nobody was injured.

PAHowever, a falling tool remains a serious hazard, so please stop your work and listen carefully.

PAFrom today, tool control is much tighter, because every tool used above floor level needs a lanyard.

PABefore you begin any task, always check your work order carefully for the new lanyard step.

PAIf an access stand feels loose or unsafe, do not use it, and report it immediately.

PAWe will hold a short toolbox talk at three o'clock, about the agreed corrective action.

PAThank you for reporting openly, because one clear report can genuinely prevent the next incident.

PAStay alert, look after each other, and remember that no deadline is ever worth an injury.

04Grammar in context, past continuous for background action

An incident interrupts a task. To report it well, you show the background action that was in progress, and then the short event that cut in. English uses the past continuous for the background, and the past simple for the interrupting event.

Look again at these lines from Dialogue 1:

“I was torquing the flap bolts when the torque wrench slipped.”
“As the wrench slipped, it dropped two metres.”

The long action (was torquing) sets the scene. The short action (slipped) interrupts it. Use when before the short event, and while or as before the long one.

Notice the difference in duration between the two forms. The past continuous describes an activity that was already in progress and still continuing, whereas the past simple describes a single, completed event that suddenly interrupted it. Because your first language has no continuous aspect, this contrast will probably feel unnatural at first, so you should practise it deliberately until the interrupted pattern becomes automatic in your own incident reports.

FormStructureExample
Positivewas / were + verb-ingI was standing on the platform.
Negativewas / were + not + verb-ingI was not using a lanyard.
Questionwas / were + subject + verb-ingWere you working on the flap?
Interruptedpast continuous + when + past simpleI was torquing the bolt when it slipped.

Common mistakes: Latvian and Russian speakers

Latvian and Russian do not have a continuous tense. So learners often use the past simple for the background, drop the -ing, or use the wrong form of be. Fix these first.

When it slipped, I torqued the panel. When it slipped, I was torquing the panel. The background task needs the past continuous, not the past simple.
We was working under the flap. We were working under the flap. Use were with we, you and they; use was with I, he, she and it.
I was work on the panel when the light failed. I was working on the panel when the light failed. Keep the -ing ending on the main verb after was or were.

07Writing task

Write a short Occurrence Report for the dropped-tool near-miss. Use the same genre as E5. Write 90–120 words. Keep facts and opinion apart. Use the past continuous for the background action.

Remember that an occurrence report is a permanent record, which other engineers, auditors and regulators may read months later. Therefore, write in short, unambiguous Simplified Technical English sentences, keep every single statement factual, and carefully avoid any language that assigns personal blame to a colleague.

Include these parts

  • Location and type of occurrence (near-miss).
  • What happened, as a clear sequence of events.
  • Immediate action you took.
  • One contributing factor and one corrective action.

Occurrence Report, OR-25-114
Location: Hangar 2, Bay 3, work platform. Type: near-miss (dropped tool).
What happened: I was torquing the flap panel bolts when my torque wrench slipped. It dropped about two metres to the floor. It landed clear of the aircraft, and no one was below.
Immediate action: I stopped the task, barriered the area, and told the safety officer at once.
Contributing factor: I was not using a lanyard, because the work order did not list one.
Corrective action: fit a lanyard to every tool above the floor, and update the work order.
