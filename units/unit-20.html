00The situation

It is simulation day at the Part-145 maintenance organisation. You have one defect on the aircraft, and the entire day belongs to the documentation that proves the rectification. Today the trail matters more than the tool.

Every certified repair generates a connected sequence of records. The tech log captures the fault, the work order authorises the job, and the task card specifies each individual step. Because every record must reference the next one accurately, an independent reader can reconstruct the whole story without questioning you.

Your objective today is to assemble that documentation for a single defect and then defend it. Initially, you compose each record in Simplified Technical English. Afterwards, you connect the records through a cross-reference, which delivers complete traceability. Finally, a mock auditor examines the trail and challenges you to justify every link.

The principle that governs the entire simulation is traceability. Traceability means an inspector can trace one defect from the discovery of the fault to the moment the aircraft returned to service. Because aviation is safety-critical, an incomplete trail is treated as a genuine defect, although the physical repair itself was faultless.

Investigators repeatedly discover that the engineering was correct, whereas the accompanying documentation was deficient. A missing signature, a broken cross-reference, or a component without a serial number can immobilise an expensive aircraft for hours. Consequently, a professional organisation trains its technicians to construct the trail with the identical discipline they apply to the repair. Read the model dialogues first, and remember to listen before you read.

01Listen, three model dialogues

Listen to each track first, with the transcript closed. Then open the transcript and read along. There is also a short briefing voicemail at the end.

Dialogue 1, Laying out the documentation trail
Transcript

AToday is the simulation day. You must build a full documentation trail for one defect.

BI found the defect this morning. The cabin door seal is worn.

AGood. First you raise a work order. Then the task card gives you the job.

BSo the work order comes first, and the task card comes after it?

AYes. The work order opens the job, and the task card lists every step.

BWhere do I record the fault itself?

AYou write the defect in the tech log, because every fault starts there.

BHow do I find the correct part for the seal?

AYou look in the IPC. The IPC is the illustrated parts catalogue.

BAnd the repair steps? Are they in the IPC too?

ANo. The repair steps are in the AMM, the aircraft maintenance manual.

BThere are so many documents. How do they stay connected?

AThrough cross-reference. Each document points to the next one, so the trail is complete.

BSo the cross-reference gives me traceability across the whole job?

AExactly. Traceability means anyone can follow the trail from find to fix.

BAnd at the end I sign the sign-off block?

AYes. The sign-off block closes each record, because it shows who did the work.

Dialogue 2, Adding traceability to the parts
Transcript

AYour trail looks clear. However, the traceability of the new part is not complete.

BWhat is missing? I fitted a new seal from the store.

AYou must record the part number. Without it, nobody can trace the part.

BThe part number is on the label. Do I need the serial number too?

AFor a tracked part, yes. The serial number identifies that one single item.

BThis seal came in a sealed bag with a batch number.

AThen record the batch number, since the seal has no serial number.

BI see. So the part number and the batch number give traceability.

ACorrect. Next, describe the rectification. Write what you did to clear the defect.

BMy corrective action was simple. I removed the old seal and fitted a new seal.

AGood. That corrective action is the rectification for this defect.

BAfter the work, a colleague checked it. Is that the dual inspection?

AYes. A dual inspection means a second person confirms the safety-critical work.

BThen I can release the aircraft?

AOnly after the release to service. That statement says the aircraft is serviceable.

BAnd I link every step with a reference number?

AYes. Each reference number ties one record to the next, so the trail holds together.

Dialogue 3, Defending the trail to the auditor
Transcript

AI am the auditor today. Show me the documentation trail for this defect.

BOf course. I start with the tech log, where I recorded the worn seal.

AGood. How do I know which part you fitted?

BThe cross-reference takes you from the tech log to the task card and the part number.

AAnd the repair itself? Where is that written down?

BThe task card cross-references the AMM, so you can follow every step.

AThis is a strong audit trail. Why does the cross-reference matter so much?

BBecause it gives traceability. Anyone can follow the trail from find to fix.

AWho confirmed the safety-critical step?

BA second technician did the dual inspection. His name is in the sign-off block.

AI can see two sign-off blocks. Why are there two?

BOne is for the work, and the other is for the inspection. Therefore both are signed.

AVery clear. As a result, I can trust this trail without asking you more.

BThat is the goal. A good documentation trail answers the question before you ask it.

AAgreed. Your trail is coherent, and every record cross-references the next one.

BThank you. I can defend every line, because the traceability is complete.

03Grammar in context, linking words for cohesion

An audit trail must demonstrate a reasoned sequence. The inspector needs to understand exactly why one step followed another. You achieve this cohesion with linking words, such as because, therefore, so, and then. Together they transform an unconnected list of facts into a coherent explanation.

Look at three lines from the dialogues. In Dialogue 3, the technician shows a result:

"One is for the work, and the other is for the inspection. Therefore both are signed."

The word therefore tells the auditor that the second fact follows from the first. In the same dialogue, the auditor draws a conclusion:

"Very clear. As a result, I can trust this trail without asking you more."

And in Dialogue 2, the mentor gives a reason with since:

"Then record the batch number, since the seal has no serial number."

Choose the linking word that matches your meaning. Do you want to give a reason, show a result, mark a sequence, or show a contrast? The table below sorts the common linking words by their job. Use one linking word per join, and keep each sentence short.

JobLinking wordsExample at level
Give a reasonbecause, since, asWe replaced the seal because it was worn.
Show a resultso, therefore, as a result, consequentlyThe seal was worn, so we replaced it.
Mark a sequencefirst, then, after that, before, afterFirst we opened the panel. Then we removed the seal.
Show a contrasthowever, although, butThe repair was correct. However, the trail was not complete.

Why linking words matter for the trail

A linking word is not decoration; within an audit trail it communicates your reasoning to the inspector. When you write "the seal was worn, therefore we replaced it", you demonstrate that the corrective action followed logically from the fault. Because an auditor evaluates the reasoning and not merely the individual facts, a precise connector eliminates a lengthy verbal explanation. Consequently, experienced authors select each connector deliberately, and they never abandon the reader to guess a relationship.

Three mistakes to avoid (Latvian / Russian speakers)

The three errors below are common, because Latvian and Russian join ideas in a slightly different way. Study each wrong line, and then fix the pattern in your own writing. Notice that the correct version always uses one linking word, not two.

Wrong ✗, Because the seal was worn, so we replaced it.
Right ✓, Because the seal was worn, we replaced it. (Use one linking word, not two.)
Wrong ✗, The valve leaked therefore we removed it.
Right ✓, The valve leaked. Therefore, we removed it. (Start a new sentence, or use "so".)
Wrong ✗, The flight was delayed because of the seal was worn.
Right ✓, The flight was delayed because the seal was worn. (Use "because" before a clause.)

Quality Audit, Finding QA-2026-018 · Documentation trail · A/C reg YL-BAM

Scope: The auditor reviewed the documentation trail for one defect, the worn cabin door seal. The repair itself was correct. However, the audit found gaps in the traceability of the record.

05Speaking, role-play

Work in pairs. Take a card each. Use the useful phrases and the linking words. Swap roles after five minutes.

Presenting a trail out loud is different from writing it, because you must explain the links in real time. During each role-play, join your facts with clear linking words, such as "because", "so", and "therefore". If your partner cannot follow a link, they should stop you at once, so that you practise defending the trail under mild pressure.

Role-play Card 1: Mentor and junior

SituationA junior starts the simulation and asks how the records connect.
Your role (A)You are the mentor. You explain the order of the documents.
Your goalExplain the trail from the tech log to the sign-off block, in order.
Useful phrases"First you raise the work order." · "Then the task card gives the steps." · "So the trail is complete."

Role-play Card 2: Technician and stores

SituationA technician calls the stores about the traceability of a new part.

Your goalConfirm the part number, and the serial number or the batch number.
Useful phrases"I need the part number." · "There is no serial number, so I record the batch number." · "That gives full traceability."

Role-play Card 3: Mock auditor and technician

SituationA mock auditor reads the finished trail and asks the technician to defend it.

06Writing task

Now construct the documentation independently. Below is the defect for today's simulation. Compose a concise documentation-trail summary that connects the records in sequence, using precise linking words. Afterwards, apply the self-check list, and peer-review a partner's version.

An effective summary allows an auditor to follow your defect from discovery to rectification without a single question. As you write, identify each record explicitly, and insert a linking word that demonstrates why one step followed the previous one. Keep every sentence short, although you must ensure that every reference number and every article remains present.

Today's defect (write the trail for this)

Aircraft YL-BAM. During the walk-around, you found a worn cabin door seal on the lower edge. You entered the defect in the tech log (TL-0912). A work order (WO-4471) was raised, and task card TC-52-11-07 was issued. You looked up the part number in the IPC and fitted a new seal (batch number B-2231, no serial number). You torqued the fasteners to the AMM value. A colleague did the dual inspection. Both sign-off blocks were signed, and the release to service was completed.

Write six to eight short lines. Use a linking word in most lines (because, so, therefore, then, after). Cover: the defect, the documents in order, the part traceability, the dual inspection, and the release to service.

Model answer (open after you write)

Defect: There is a worn cabin door seal on YL-BAM. The defect is recorded in the tech log, entry TL-0912.

Documents: A work order (WO-4471) was raised, so task card TC-52-11-07 was issued. The task card cross-references the AMM for the steps.

Traceability: The part number came from the IPC. The seal has no serial number, so the batch number (B-2231) was recorded.

Corrective action: The old seal was removed, and a new seal was fitted. Therefore the rectification is complete.

Close-out: A dual inspection was done, because the door is safety-critical. Both sign-off blocks were signed. Consequently, the release to service was completed, and the aircraft is serviceable.
