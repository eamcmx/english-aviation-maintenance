01Scenario

Today is a simulation day. You and a partner work as avionics technicians in a Part-145 hangar. A service bulletin asks you to modify the number-two radio on aircraft MSN 4471. You must run the whole job in sequence: read the paperwork, change the wiring, load the software, test the system and sign it off. This is real configuration control, so every step is traced. Work slowly, read each reference aloud, and record any deviation before you release the aircraft.

You are the certifying team on the late shift. The task cards are signed out to you. One of you drives the tools; the other reads the references and watches the screen. Nothing is embodied until the tech log entry is written and the sign-off is stamped.

Why do we practise the whole job as one sequence? Because a real embodiment fails when the steps drift out of order. If you skip the effectivity check, you may modify the wrong aircraft. If you load the software before the wiring is proven, you waste an hour when continuity is bad. Therefore, in this simulation, you keep the paperwork, the tools and the test in strict order, and you speak each step aloud so your partner can catch a mistake early.

How this simulation runs

You take the job through five stages: documentation, wiring, software load, functional test, and completion. At the end of each stage, one partner reports what is done, while the other confirms it against the reference. Although the language stays simple, the discipline is real: unless a stage is confirmed, you do not move on. When a fault appears, you decide together whether it is an accepted deviation or a genuine snag that stops the release.

04Grammar in context, present perfect for reported completion

When you report a job as done, English uses the present perfect: have or has plus the past participle. The embodiment is an achieved result, so you say what has been done, not when. Look at these lines from the dialogues and the voicemail:

"We have embodied the modification."
"I have raised the work order in the system."
"I have recorded the deviation on the work order."

The pattern is simple. The subject chooses have or has. Then you add the third form of the verb, the past participle. You use it for a finished action with a result now. You do not add a finished-time word, such as yesterday.

Subjecthave / hasPast participleExample
I / You / We / TheyhaveembodiedWe have embodied the modification.
He / She / IthasloadedThe technician has loaded the software.
QuestionHave / Has+ subject + participleHave you signed the tech log?
Negativehave not / has not+ participleWe have not found any other snag.

Three common mistakes (Latvian / Russian speakers)

1. Do not drop the auxiliary have.
✗ We already embodied the modification.
✓ We have already embodied the modification.
Latvian and Russian have no present perfect, so learners drop have. Keep it for a result now.
2. Use the correct third form of the verb.
✗ The technician has wrote the entry.
✓ The technician has written the entry.
The present perfect uses the past participle (written, done, taken), not the past simple (wrote, did, took).
3. Do not use the present perfect with a finished time.
✗ We have finished the test yesterday.
✓ We finished the test yesterday.
With a finished-time word, such as yesterday, use the past simple. Keep the present perfect for "the result is now".

06Speaking, role-plays

Work with a partner, or use the AI conversation partner below. Keep your sentences short. Read every reference aloud and confirm it, just like in the dialogues.

Role-play 1: Plan the job

Situation A service bulletin asks you to modify the number-two radio. You have the task cards.
Your role Lead technician (A).
Your goal Check the effectivity, confirm the part number, and agree the sequence with your partner.
Useful phrases "Let me check the effectivity." · "Do you confirm the part number?" · "The wiring card comes first." · "This is configuration control."

Role-play 2: Do the wiring

Situation You must change one connector and test continuity before the software load.
Your role Assistant technician (B).
Your goal Follow the wiring diagram, route the harness, and prove continuity on every pin.
Useful phrases "Handle the pins with care." · "The wiring diagram shows an extra wire." · "Continuity is good." · "Shall I mate the connector?"

Role-play 3: Test and report a deviation

Situation The functional test passes, but the BITE shows a snag: antenna gain minus three.
Your role Lead technician (A).
Your goal Decide if it is inside the limit, then record the deviation on the work order.
Useful phrases "The BITE shows a snag." · "Is it inside the limit?" · "This is an accepted deviation." · "Record it on the work order."

Role-play 4: Hand over and sign off

Situation Your shift ends. The embodiment is done, but the release still needs a review.
Your role Off-going technician.
Your goal Report completion: what you have embodied, the deviation, and what is left before sign-off.
Useful phrases "We have embodied the modification." · "The modification status is post-mod." · "Please review the deviation." · "The tech log is ready."

07Writing, a completion report in Simplified Technical English

Write the completion report for the embodiment you did in the dialogues. Use ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English habits: short sentences (no more than 20 words), one idea per sentence, the active voice, and simple approved words. Do not use two words when one is enough.

Your task

Write a completion report of 70–100 words. Report the job as done, so use the present perfect where you can. Include: the service bulletin and the aircraft, the wiring change and the software load, the functional test and the BITE result, the accepted deviation, the modification status, and the sign-off. End with your name and the date.

STE tips: write "We have embodied the service bulletin", not "The embodiment of the bulletin has been accomplished". Write "The load is complete", not "The loading procedure has been finalised". Keep each sentence to one idea.
