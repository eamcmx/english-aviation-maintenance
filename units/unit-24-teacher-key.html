2 First-language interference — why each error happens

Latvian and Russian speakers share several predictable traps in this unit. Each note gives the cause, the typical wrong output, and the quick fix to drill.

Latvian Russian

A. No present perfect in the first language

Neither Latvian nor Russian has a have + participle tense. Both use a single past form for "we did it" and "we have done it". So learners report a finished job with the past simple and drop the auxiliary.

✗ "We already embodied the modification."  →  ✓ "We have already embodied the modification."

Why it matters here: the whole unit reports results (the embodiment is done, the deviation is recorded), so the present perfect is the natural register. Drill: "result now → have/has + participle". Insist on the auxiliary in every sign-off sentence.

Russian Latvian

B. Verb aspect mapped onto the wrong English tense

Russian marks perfective vs imperfective aspect on the verb itself; Latvian leans on prefixes and adverbs. Learners feel "completed = one perfective form" and then attach an English time word to it, producing the classic present-perfect-with-finished-time error.

✗ "We have finished the test yesterday."  →  ✓ "We finished the test yesterday."

Fix: teach the rule as a switch — a finished-time word (yesterday, at 14:00, on the late shift) forces the past simple; no time word plus a result now allows the present perfect.

Latvian Russian

C. Irregular past participles

Because the tense is new, learners over-regularise or reuse the past simple as the participle: has wrote, has did, has taken vs correct forms. This unit hits write→written, do→done, find→found directly.

Drill: a short participle list on the board — write/written, do/done, find/found, take/taken, sign/signed, load/loaded. E6 targets exactly these.

Latvian Russian

D. Missing articles

Neither language has a/the. Learners drop them: "Confirm part number before you fit connector." In documentation this reads as clipped STE, so it feels acceptable — but in speech it sounds off.

Fix: accept article-light style in the written STE task, but require articles in the spoken role-plays: "Confirm the part number before you fit the connector."

Russian

E. "Make" vs "do", and pronunciation of specialist terms

Russian делать covers both make and do, so learners say "make the functional test". Correct: "run / do the functional test", "carry out the modification". Also watch stress: de-VI-ation, ef-fec-TIV-ity, and that BITE is said as the word "bite".

Fix: teach the fixed collocations as chunks (run a test, raise a work order, record a deviation) rather than free word choice.

3 Extension activities

  • Reverse the deviation. Change the BITE reading to minus six. Pairs re-run the sign-off: now the reading is outside the limit, so the aircraft is rejected and the job is not released. Learners must switch from "accepted deviation" language to "raise a snag / reject" language.
  • Second bulletin, mechanical. Give a one-line SB for a bracket replacement (torque value, part number). Learners write the task-card steps in STE and report completion — extends the unit to airframe/mechanic work, not only avionics.
  • Tech-log dictation. Read a completion entry at natural pace; learners write it, then compare with a partner. Trains comprehension of spoken part numbers and minus figures (links to the numerical quiz items).
  • Shift-handover voicemail. Each learner records a 40-second handover like Track 4: what they have embodied, the deviation, what is left. Peers listen and note the three facts.
  • STE rewrite race. Provide five over-long, passive sentences. Pairs rewrite each into one-idea active STE under 20 words. Fastest correct set wins.

4 CEFR B2 speaking rubric — role-plays

Score each learner 1–5 on the five dimensions during the role-plays. B2 is the target band (a "4"). Total /25.

Range Uses the full bank and varied structures with ease; precise part-number and deviation language. Uses most bank terms and their collocations correctly; enough range to run the whole stage. Systematic tense error; listener must work to follow.
Fluency Smooth, near-natural pace; hesitation only for genuine thought. Keeps the job moving; short natural pauses at stage changes. Noticeable hesitation; some reformulation slows the exchange. Long pauses break the flow of the task.
Interaction Leads and supports the partner; confirms references and hands over turns naturally. Reads references back and confirms; asks and answers to keep the sequence on track. Responds when prompted but rarely initiates the confirm-back. Waits to be led; little turn-taking or confirmation.
Coherence Full stage sequenced with varied connectors (as soon as, unless, provided). Clear step order with common connectors (then, because, so, before). Steps present but loosely linked; some out-of-order. Steps jumbled; the sequence is hard to follow.

Quick note for feedback: praise one thing they did (usually confirm-back or a clean present perfect), then name one fixable target from §2.
