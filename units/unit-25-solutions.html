Solutions — Exercises E1–E8 + model writing answer

Reporting a near-miss or hangar incident. Full answers with a one-line reason for each, plus the annotated model Occurrence Report.

These are complete model answers for marking. Each item shows the accepted answer and a one-line reason. Where the student page accepts alternatives, they are shown with a slash (/).

E1 Gap-fill — put the right term in each gap

Bank terms placed by meaning in context.

  1. near-miss — the tool fell but hit no one, so it almost caused harm.
  2. lanyard — a cord ties the tool to you so it cannot fall.
  3. root cause — the first, real reason a problem happened.
  4. safety officer — the person who manages safety reports.
  5. hazard — a loose stand is something that could cause harm.
  6. debrief — a short meeting to review what happened.
  7. corrective action — a step that stops the problem happening again.
  8. contributing factor — it helped cause the event but was not the main reason.
  9. FOD — a loose tool left behind is foreign object debris.
  10. tech log — the aircraft's running record of defects and actions.

E2 Matching — term to meaning

1 B · 2 C · 3 D · 4 E · 5 F · 6 G · 7 H · 8 A

  1. preventive action → B — a step taken before a problem, to stop it starting.
  2. lanyard → C — a cord that ties a tool to you.
  3. mitigation → D — an action that lowers a risk.
  4. tool control → E — the system for tracking every tool.
  5. observation → F — a fact you saw, not an opinion.
  6. work order → G — a document that authorises and records a task.
  7. torque wrench → H — a tool that tightens a bolt to an exact force.
  8. sequence of events → A — the order in which things happened.

E3 Dialogue completion — complete the report talk

One word per gap.

  1. report — the fixed phrase is "report a near-miss".
  2. hurt — the first check is always injury: "is anyone hurt?"
  3. stopped — past simple; the mechanic stopped the task at once.
  4. opinion — "an observation, not an opinion" is the register rule.
  5. torquing — past continuous background action after "was".
  6. standing — "where were you standing?" — position at the moment.
  7. dropped / fell — past simple for the short event; both are accepted.
  8. debrief — the short review meeting held after the event.

E4 Grammar transformation — make the past continuous

was / were + -ing.

  1. was torquing — "I" takes "was"; keep the -ing.
  2. were working — "we" takes "were".
  3. was standing — "she" takes "was".
  4. were climbing — "they" takes "were".
  5. was not using — negative: was + not + -ing.
  6. Were you checking — question: were + subject + -ing.
  7. was falling — background action in progress as I reached out.
  8. was writing — the ongoing action interrupted by Rita's arrival.

E5 Reading 1 — an Occurrence Report

Short factual answers from the report.

  1. near-miss (dropped tool) — the "Type" line states it.
  2. two metres / 2 — "dropped about two metres to the floor".
  3. no — "No person was below at the time".
  4. the torque wrench — named in "What happened".
  5. e.g. no lanyard / poor light / loose stand / work order gap — any one from the "Contributing factors" line.
  6. the tech log — the loose rail was recorded there last week.

E6 Error-spotting — fix the flawed report

Eight fixes: grammar, and opinion mixed with fact.

  1. was work → was working — keep the -ing after "was".
  2. We was → were standing — "we" takes "were".
  3. I am torquing → was torquing — past, not present; it is a past report.
  4. loose — keep the fact; drop the opinion words "stupid / useless".
  5. clear off → clear of — the correct preposition is "clear of".
  6. opinion — replace blame with "I have kept my opinion out of the timeline".
  7. They was → were not using — "they" takes "were".
  8. the — "to the safety officer" needs the article.

  2. a lanyard — Step 1: clip a lanyard to each hand tool.

E8 Listening review — questions on Dialogue 3

Answers from Audio 3.

  1. a dropped tool — the near-miss was a dropped-tool event.
  2. a lanyard — every tool above floor level must have one now.
  3. better lighting — installed under the flap as a mitigation.
  4. poor light — the second contributing factor named.
  5. the work order — updated so it requires a lanyard check.
  6. before the task / job — tool control now starts before work begins.
  7. a toolbox talk — Rita will hold one this week.
  8. a person — the plan fixes the system, and never blames a person.

WT Writing task — model Occurrence Report (annotated)

90–120 words, Simplified Technical English, facts apart from opinion, past continuous for the background.

Occurrence Report, OR-25-114
Location: Hangar 2, Bay 3, work platform. Type: near-miss (dropped tool).
What happened: I was torquing the flap panel bolts when my torque wrench slipped. It dropped about two metres to the floor. It landed clear of the aircraft, and no one was below.
Immediate action: I stopped the task, barriered the area, and told the safety officer at once.
Contributing factor: I was not using a lanyard, because the work order did not list one.
Corrective action: fit a lanyard to every tool above the floor, and update the work order.

Sequence of events. The story is told in order — torquing, slip, fall, landing — so an auditor can follow it. Full marks for a clear, ordered timeline.
Past continuous. "was torquing" and "was not using" mark the long background actions; "slipped" and "dropped" are past simple for the short events. This is the target grammar.
Observation vs opinion. Every line states a fact. No blame words ("careless", "his fault", "stupid stand"). This is the register rule for the genre.
Simplified Technical English. Short sentences, one idea each, active voice, no vague words — the writing habit taught in the unit.
Actions. One contributing factor (no lanyard) and one corrective action (fit a lanyard, update the work order) are both present, as the brief requires.

Marking guide: award for (1) location + type, (2) ordered sequence of events, (3) immediate action, (4) one contributing factor, (5) one corrective action, (6) correct past continuous, (7) no opinion or blame. A strong answer meets all seven inside 90–120 words.
