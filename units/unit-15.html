MEL dispatch: the duty-engineer call.

A jet has an inoperative item. Can it still fly? In this unit you read the MEL, weigh the conditions and limitations, fit the placard, and tell the duty engineer what the operational and maintenance procedures require — all in clear, spoken B2 English.

ScenarioDuty-engineer dispatch call VocabularyMEL & dispatch — 20 terms GrammarModals of permission & possibility Class length90 minutes · balanced skills

By the end of this unit you can …

  • Can interpret an MEL item's conditions, limitations and procedures.
  • Can discuss whether an aircraft can dispatch under the MEL.
  • Can state the operational and maintenance procedures the item requires.

01The situation

It is night at a busy line station. An A320 is on stand, ready to fly, but the crew has written up a fault in the technical log. One item is inoperative. The turnaround clock is running, and the captain wants an answer: can we go?

You cannot decide alone. You call the duty engineer. Together you open the Minimum Equipment List, read the conditions, and agree the go or no-go. Then you fit the placard, defer the defect, and sign the release. In this unit you learn to hold that conversation with confidence.

In plain words — four terms before you start

MEL (Minimum Equipment List) — the book that says which items may be broken and still let the aircraft fly. Inoperative — not working. Dispatch — release the aircraft to fly. Placard — a small warning label you fit near a broken control. Every hard word in this unit is glossed in the vocabulary bank.

02Listen — three duty-engineer calls

Listen to each call first. Do not read yet. Then open the transcript and check the words. The same voices come back in the exercises, so listen for the conditions, the limitations and the procedures.

Dialogue 1 — One air-conditioning pack inoperative

A = Kārlis, line technician (mechanical) · B = Rob, duty engineer

Transcript

KārlisDuty desk, it's Kārlis on stand twelve. I have a problem on the A320.

RobGo ahead, Kārlis. What is the defect?

KārlisThe number two air-conditioning pack is inoperative. The crew wrote it up in the technical log.

RobUnderstood. Did you get a fault code from the BITE test?

KārlisYes. The pack flow-control valve fails to open. It looks like a valve fault, not the controller.

RobRight. Let's open the minimum equipment list and see if we can dispatch.

KārlisI have the MEL page here. It says one pack may be inoperative.

RobGood. That is a repair category C item. So the rectification interval is ten calendar days.

KārlisSo the aircraft may dispatch with one pack inoperative?

RobYes, but only if you meet the provisos. Read me the conditions.

KārlisCondition one is a limitation on altitude. The flight level must not go above three one zero.

RobCorrect. A single pack cannot pressurise the cabin above that level.

KārlisCondition two — we must placard the pack control on the overhead panel.

RobDo that now. Fit an "INOP" placard next to the switch so the crew sees it.

KārlisUnderstood. And the maintenance procedure?

RobPull and collar the pack circuit breaker, then raise the deferred defect in the tech log.

KārlisCopy. I will sign the certificate of release to service after placarding.

RobOne more thing. Brief the crew on the operational procedure before they leave.

KārlisI will brief them. Thanks, Rob. I'll call you when the placard is on.

Dialogue 2 — Weather radar inoperative, and a missing part

A = Inga, avionics technician · B = Rob, duty engineer

Transcript

IngaHi Rob, it's Inga at the line station. I have an avionics defect on the 737.

RobHello Inga. I am the duty engineer tonight. What is unserviceable?

IngaThe weather radar is inoperative. The display is blank and the BITE shows a transmitter fault.

RobLet me check the minimum equipment list. Is the second radar serviceable?

IngaThere is only one radar on this aircraft. So we have no backup.

RobThen read the MEL item with me. Weather radar — dispatch conditions.

IngaThe repair category is C. The rectification interval is ten days.

RobWhat is the main limitation in the proviso?

IngaThe aircraft must not fly into known or forecast thunderstorm areas.

RobCorrect. The crew must avoid convective weather. That is the operational procedure.

IngaUnderstood. Do I placard the radar control?

RobYes. Fit an "INOP" placard on the radar panel, then defer the defect.

IngaThere is one more thing. A static wick is missing from the wingtip.

RobThat is not an MEL item. Check the configuration deviation list for it.

IngaThe configuration deviation list allows dispatch with one static wick missing.

RobGood. You need no placard for that, but note it in the technical log.

IngaSo can the aircraft dispatch tonight?

RobYes, if you placard the radar and record both items. Then sign the release.

IngaI will raise the deferred defect and call you before I sign the certificate of release to service.

RobPerfect. Keep me posted from the line station.

Dialogue 3 — APU inoperative: is it a no-go item?

A = Toms, line supervisor · B = Rob, duty engineer

Transcript

TomsRob, it's Toms. The APU on the A320 won't start. Is this a no-go item?

RobNot automatically. Let's read the minimum equipment list before we decide.

TomsThe APU is inoperative. The crew tried three starts with no light-up.

RobOur MEL is based on the master minimum equipment list from the manufacturer.

TomsI see the APU item. The repair category is B. So we have three days.

RobYes. The rectification interval for a category B item is three calendar days.

TomsSo the aircraft may dispatch tonight without the APU?

RobIt may, but check the provisos. A ground air source must be available.

TomsWe have a ground cart at each line station on tonight's route.

RobGood. Then it is a go item, not a no-go item.

TomsWhat about engine start? The APU normally supplies the bleed air.

RobThe operational procedure is a cross-bleed start or an air start from the cart.

TomsUnderstood. I will placard the APU switch and defer the defect.

RobAlso brief the incoming crew. The deferred defect must be visible in the technical log.

TomsCopy. I'll write the limitation and the procedure on the deferral page.

RobAnd watch the rectification interval. It expires in three days, so plan the fix.

TomsCan I sign the certificate of release to service now?

RobYes, once the placard is on and the tech log is complete. Then release it.

TomsThanks Rob. The aircraft is serviceable for dispatch with the APU deferred.

04Grammar in context — modals of permission & possibility

Dispatch hinges on what is allowed and what is possible. So this unit lives on modal verbs: may, can, could, must not, cannot. They tell the crew what they are permitted to do and what they must never do. Listen to how the duty engineer uses them.

"So the aircraft may dispatch with one pack inoperative?" — permission granted by the MEL.

"The fault could be in the valve, not the controller." — a possibility, not a fact.

"The flight level must not go above three one zero." — a firm prohibition.

"A single pack cannot pressurise the cabin above that level." — a physical impossibility.

A key rule: a modal is followed by the bare verb, with no "to". We say may dispatch, never may to dispatch. And modals do not stack: we cannot say will can. For future ability we use will be able to.

FunctionModalExample from the calls
Permission (allowed)may / canThe aircraft may dispatch with one pack inoperative.
Possibility (maybe)could / mayThe fault could be in the valve.
Prohibition (not allowed)must not / may notThe flight must not climb above FL310.
Future abilitywill be able toWe will be able to fix it tomorrow.

Three mistakes to avoid (Latvian & Russian speakers)

Why it happensWrongRight
You add "to" after the modal (as in your first language).The aircraft may to dispatch.The aircraft may dispatch.
You drop the article "the" (there is no article in Latvian or Russian).Aircraft can dispatch if crew follows procedure.The aircraft can dispatch if the crew follows the procedure.
You stack two modals for the future.Tomorrow we will can sign the release.Tomorrow we will be able to sign the release.

MEL Item 21-52-01 · Air-Conditioning Pack (one)

Repair category: C — rectification interval ten (10) calendar days.

Number installed: 2. Number required for dispatch: 1.

One air-conditioning pack may be inoperative provided that:

(a) the affected pack is deactivated and the crew is briefed;
(b) the flight level does not exceed FL310 (limitation on cabin pressurisation);
(c) the pack control switch is placarded "INOP".

07Writing — a deferred-defect entry

Write a deferred-defect entry for the technical log. Use Simplified Technical English (ASD-STE100): short sentences, one instruction each, the active voice, and approved words. Keep each sentence under about twenty words.

Task

A single VHF communication radio (VHF 2) is inoperative on an A320. The MEL item is repair category C (rectification interval ten days). The proviso is that at least one VHF radio works and the crew is briefed. Write the deferred-defect entry: state the defect, the MEL reference, the limitation, the placard, and both procedures. Finish with the release. Write 90–120 words in STE.

Show a model answer

DEFERRED DEFECT — VHF 2 INOPERATIVE

Defect: VHF 2 is inoperative. VHF 1 is serviceable.

MEL reference: 23-12-02, repair category C. Rectification interval: 10 calendar days.

Proviso: one VHF radio must work. The crew must use VHF 1.

Limitation: none on the route. Placard: fit "INOP" on the VHF 2 control.

(M) Maintenance procedure: deactivate VHF 2. Raise the deferred defect in the technical log.

(O) Operational procedure: brief the crew. The crew must use VHF 1 for all calls.

Release: sign the certificate of release to service after the placard is on.
