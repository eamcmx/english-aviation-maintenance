2 · Latvian & Russian interference — why each error happens

These three are predictable, not careless. Name the cause; the fix then becomes a conscious habit.

A · “In first / in the end” for the sequencer

✗ In first, read the task card.   ✓ First, read the task card.

Why: Latvian (vispirms / sākumā) and Russian (сначала / в первую очередь) express “first of all” with a preposition-like frame, so learners add a spare in. The same logic produces in the end for the last step — but in the end means “eventually” in English, not “as the final step”. Fix: the bare adverbs — First, Finally. Drill them as fixed one-word signals.

B · Dropped articles before a known part

✗ Put wheel on axle.   ✓ Put the wheel on the axle.

Why: neither Latvian nor Russian has articles, so there is no first-language cue to insert the. Under the time pressure of speaking, it is the first thing to disappear — especially before a technical noun the learner is focused on (wheel, axle, nut, jack). Fix: teach the rule “a known part on the aircraft takes the”, and correct every dropped article in E4, E6 and the writing task, because in an instruction the article carries real meaning (a specific part, not any part).

C · Continuous or added subject for a command

✗ Then you are torquing the nut.   ✓ Then, torque the nut.

Why: Russian and Latvian mark a command with mood and aspect on the verb, not by dropping the subject, so learners keep a subject (you) and reach for a “process” form that looks like the English continuous. Fix: contrast the three English uses — statement (you torque), process (you are torquing), instruction (torque) — and reserve the bare imperative for a teaching step. A short imperative is also safer: one clear action the junior can follow.

Also watch: “must to pull” (no to after a modal — see E6/8); word stress on COMpetence and RAtionale; and /w/ vs /v/ in wrench, wheel, walk-through for Russian speakers.

3 · Extension activities (fast finishers / longer class)

  • Reverse walk-through. The junior (stronger learner) teaches the mentor a step and deliberately plants one error; the mentor must catch it and correct it constructively — show the fault, give the reason.
  • Genre swap. Turn the writing task into a proper task card: number the steps, add the AMM reference, the torque value (300 Nm) and a sign-off line. Marks Simplified Technical English discipline.
  • Avionics transfer. Re-tell a coaching sequence for reading a BITE readout (self-test → fault code → fault-isolation manual → action), so the sequencer + imperative pattern crosses to avionics work.
  • Comprehension-check tennis. In threes, one learner gives a step, the next asks a “say-it-back” check, the third gives the rationale — rotate at speed.
  • Training-record write-up. After the role-plays, each “mentor” dictates a two-line entry for the OJT record (like E7): what was taught, what was corrected, whether ready for assessment.
  • Correction register. Rank three ways to correct the lockwire error from bluntest to kindest; discuss why “show the fault + give the reason” builds competence best.

4 · CEFR B2 speaking rubric — the role-plays

Score two or three pairs. Five criteria; band descriptors calibrated to the mentoring task (teach a step, check understanding, correct an error, give a rationale).

CriterionB2+ strongB2 at levelB1 below
Range
vocabulary & structure		 Uses the unit terms and collocations naturally (give a walk-through, protect airworthiness); varied sequencers; some subordination (because, unless, as soon as). Uses most target terms and 3–4 sequencers correctly; imperatives formed well; occasional repetition. Few target terms; relies on and then; imperatives often malformed or replaced by statements.
Accuracy
grammar & the target form		 Bare imperatives consistent; articles correct on known parts; the L1 slips (in first, dropped the, continuous) absent. Target form mostly correct; one or two L1 slips that do not block meaning; self-corrects some. Frequent L1 slips; you are …-ing for commands; dropped articles reduce clarity.
Fluency Steady pace across a full step sequence; hesitation only for genuine planning; no long stalls. Generally smooth in short turns; some pausing at the harder terms; keeps going. Frequent, long pauses; sequence breaks down; needs prompting to continue.
Interaction
checking & correcting		 Runs genuine comprehension checks (say-it-back), corrects the error by showing the fault + giving the reason, invites the partner to fix it. Asks a check question and corrects the error, though the reason may be thin; turn-taking works. Says only “OK?” or “that’s wrong”; no reason given; little real exchange.
Coherence
order & rationale		 Steps in correct fixed order, clearly signposted; links at least one step to airworthiness explicitly. Order mostly correct and signposted; one rationale offered when asked. Steps out of order or unsignposted; no rationale; listener would struggle to follow.

Use: a learner at B2 at level on Accuracy, Interaction and Coherence has met outcomes 1–3. Give one “keep” and one “improve” per pair in the delayed-feedback slot.
