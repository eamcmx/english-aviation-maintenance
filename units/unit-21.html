Coordinating a functional/operational test

Two people, one moving control surface, and a manual that must be followed word for word. In this unit you learn to brief the test, call and confirm each action, and read the pass/fail result, safely and clearly.

By the end of this unit you can …

  • Can brief a partner on a functional test's steps and safety precautions.
  • Can call and confirm test actions clearly while the test runs.
  • Can state and interpret the pass/fail criteria.

01The situation

It is the late shift in Hangar 2. Aircraft D-AXLR is on jacks for a flap system check. The task card in the AMM asks for a flap functional test with two people.

One technician reads the manual and calls each action out loud. The other technician stands clear, watches the flaps, and confirms each step. The flaps are heavy control surfaces. They move fast under hydraulic pressure, so the safety words matter as much as the test itself. At the end, the two of you compare the result against the pass criteria and decide: pass or fail. If it fails, you raise a snag and the aircraft stays on the ground.

This coordination is a professional skill in itself, because a functional test is never a solo activity. The technician who calls the actuation must communicate precisely, while the observer confirms exactly what the control surface actually does. Although the procedure looks simple, a single unclear callout can send a person into a hazard. Therefore you brief the safety precautions before you touch the flap lever, and you repeat them whenever the situation changes. As soon as an indication looks abnormal, you stop, unless the AMM tells you otherwise. The language of the test is deliberately short, direct, and unambiguous.

02Listen: three model dialogues

Listen first. Keep the transcript closed. Then open it and read.

Each dialogue demonstrates the same test from a different perspective: the preparatory briefing, the live coordination, and the interpretation of the result. Listen for the connectors, because they carry the professional reasoning behind every instruction. Dialogue 1, The pre-test brief (before you start)
Transcript, Dialogue 1

AWe run the flap functional test now, so I will brief you before we begin.

BUnderstood. Which AMM task card covers this operational test?

ATask card 27-51-00, which lists every procedure and each safety precaution.

BCopy. Before we start, what exactly is the pass criteria?

ABoth flaps must reach full deflection together, with no visible asymmetry.

BSo I monitor the control surface for asymmetry throughout the actuation?

ACorrect. You observe the left flap, while I call each actuation from the manual.

BUnderstood. Do we need hydraulic pressure before the first actuation?

AYes, because the actuator cannot move unless the green system is pressurised.

BAnd the BITE check, do we complete it before the functional test?

AWe run BITE first, since it confirms the actuator is serviceable.

BClear. One safety precaution: do not stand near the flaps during travel.

ACorrect. Stay behind the placard line, and keep your clearance at all times.

BShould I fit the lockout on the other system before we continue?

AYes, fit the lockout tag now, in case somebody pressurises it accidentally.

BThe lockout is fitted, and I am positioned clear. Call the first actuation whenever ready.

Dialogue 2, Running the test (call and confirm)
Transcript, Dialogue 2

APressure is up and the green system is normal. Ready for the first callout?

BReady. I am positioned clear of the flaps, so go ahead.

AFlaps to five. Actuating now. Confirm the left flap begins to move.

BLeft flap moving smoothly. The deflection looks even, with no asymmetry yet.

AGood. Flaps to fifteen. Watch the limit switch indication as it travels.

BThe limit switch made at fifteen, and the indication light is green.

AFlaps to full deflection. This actuation is the largest, so stand well clear.

BI am clear. Both control surfaces are approaching full deflection together.

AHold there. Does either flap lag behind, or is there any asymmetry?

BNo lag at all. The travel is symmetric, and both flaps match within limits.

ACopy. Flaps up now. Confirm the hydraulic pressure remains normal throughout.

BPressure is normal throughout, and the flaps are returning together without a snag.

AGood callout. The actuator sounds smooth, with no unusual noise or judder.

BAgreed. It looks serviceable to me, and the rigging appears correctly adjusted.

AOne more cycle to confirm, before we compare the result against the pass criteria.

BUnderstood. I remain clear, so call the actuation as soon as you are ready.

Dialogue 3, Reading the result (pass or fail?)
Transcript, Dialogue 3

AThis is the final cycle. Flaps to full, and watch both control surfaces closely.

BActuating. Wait, the left flap is lagging behind the right one.

AStop the test and select flaps up. Confirm that you can see asymmetry.

BConfirmed. The left flap trails by several degrees at full deflection.

AThat fails the pass criteria, unless the AMM allows a small tolerance.

BThe AMM allows no visible asymmetry, so this result is definitely a fail.

AAgreed. Do not sign it serviceable, because we must raise a snag first.

BI will record the snag in the tech log. What is the likely cause?

APossibly a rigging error, or a sluggish actuator on the left side.

BCould the limit switch be late? The indication seemed to arrive slowly.

APossibly. The BITE readout may show a fault code, so let me check it.

BWhile you check the readout, I will keep everybody clear of the flaps.

ABITE shows a fault on the left actuation channel, which fits the symptom.

BSo it is the actuator, or its wiring. I will note that in the snag.

AGood. Keep the lockout fitted, because the aircraft stays unserviceable tonight.

BUnderstood. I will add the placard and update the work order accordingly.

These terms belong to the vocabulary of testing, so learn them as active language, not as a passive list. During a functional test, you must produce them instantly, because the actuation will not wait for you. Notice how the collocations combine: you run a functional test, you meet the pass criteria, and you raise a snag whenever the result is unacceptable. Whenever you describe a control surface, be specific about the deflection, the asymmetry, and the indication you observed. Precise terminology prevents confusion between the two technicians, and confusion during a live test is genuinely dangerous.

04Grammar in context, the imperative with safety warnings

A live test moves real surfaces. So the manual and the technician give short, direct orders. This is the imperative. You use the base verb, with no subject.

Look at these lines from the dialogues. Each one is an imperative. Notice how short and clear it is.

"Do not stand near the flaps during travel.", Dialogue 1
"Stay behind the placard line. Keep your clearance at all times.", Dialogue 1
"Stop the test. Flaps up. Confirm that you see asymmetry.", Dialogue 3

To make an order, use the base verb: Stand clear. Confirm the step. Fit the lockout. To make a warning, put Do not before the base verb: Do not stand near the flaps. Do not touch the actuator. Say the warning first, then the action. A warning protects the person; the order tells them what to do.

In this professional register, the imperative is not impolite; it is efficient, because the actuation happens quickly and a long sentence wastes time. Notice that Simplified Technical English prefers one instruction per sentence, so you avoid joining two orders with a comma. You may add a short reason with because, in case, or unless, provided the sentence stays clear. For example: "Do not pressurise the system, in case the flap moves unexpectedly." The auxiliary do is compulsory in a negative order, although many learners omit it under pressure. When you rehearse the callouts aloud, the correct form becomes automatic.

FormPatternExampleWhen you use it
Positive orderbase verb (+ object)Fit the lockout.tell your partner to act
Negative warningDo not + base verbDo not stand near the flaps.stop a dangerous action
Short safety callbase verb + adverbStand well clear.a fast call during the test

Three common mistakes (Latvian and Russian speakers)

Wrong, Not stand near the flaps.
Right, Do not stand near the flaps. (Add "Do" for a negative order. English needs the auxiliary "do".)
Wrong, No touch the actuator.
Right, Do not touch the actuator. ("No + verb" comes from the first language. Use "Do not + verb".)
Wrong, You stand clear now!
Right, Stand clear now! (An English imperative has no subject. Drop "you".)

E5 · Reading 1 (AMM task card)

Read the task card, then answer

AMM Task 27-51-00-710-801, Flap System Functional Test

Warning: Do not stand near the flaps during the test. The control surfaces move fast and can crush.

1. Fit a lockout on the green hydraulic system before you begin.

2. Set the flap lever to zero. Confirm that the flaps are up.

3. Apply hydraulic pressure. Check that the indication is normal and green.

4. Run the BITE check. Confirm that the actuator reports serviceable.

5. Call each actuation from this card. Your partner confirms each step.

6. Extend the flaps to five, then fifteen, then full deflection.

7. Watch both flaps. There must be no asymmetry between the two control surfaces.

8. The limit switch must make at each stop.

Pass criteria: both flaps reach full deflection together, with no visible asymmetry, and every indication is normal. If the test fails, do not sign the aircraft serviceable. Raise a snag and fit a placard.

This task card is authentic in structure, although the numbers are simplified for practice. Read every warning first, because the sequence deliberately protects both technicians during the actuation. Notice that each subtask is a single, unambiguous instruction, which is the essence of Simplified Technical English.

07Writing, a tech log snag entry

The test failed. Write the snag entry for the tech log. Use Simplified Technical English (STE): short sentences, one order per sentence, and a simple, approved word for each idea.

Write 50–70 words. Say what failed, what you saw, the safety action, and the next step. Use the imperative for the warnings.

Simplified Technical English exists precisely to remove ambiguity from maintenance documentation. It restricts the vocabulary, shortens the sentences, and enforces one instruction per sentence, so that a non-native reader interprets the procedure identically to a native reader. When you write the snag entry, apply those habits deliberately: choose the approved word, keep the sentence short, and separate every order. A disciplined entry protects the next technician and satisfies the auditor.

Plan your entry

Line 1: the test and the result. Line 2: what you saw (the asymmetry). Line 3: the safety action (lockout, placard). Line 4: the next step (inspect, re-run). Keep each sentence short.

Show a model answer
SNAG 4471, D-AXLR. The flap functional test failed. At full deflection, the left flap lags the right flap. There is visible asymmetry between the control surfaces.
