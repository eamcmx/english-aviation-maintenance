The situation · 07:00, Part-145 hangar

A near-miss on the late shift

A technician took a phone call during a flap inspection. He was distracted. He left a spanner near a control cable.

A colleague found it during a cross-check. No damage. It was a near-miss, not an accident.

Your job today: lead the five-minute talk that stops it happening again.

Choose the modal by strength

Drag from soft advice to a firm rule.
Grammar · the one rule + three traps

Modal + base verb. No "to", no "-s".

We must to check the torque.

We must check the torque.

You not should take a call.

You should not take a call.

He should checks the lockwire.

He should check the lockwire.

Why these three?

+to — Latvian/Russian link two verbs with a marker; English drops it.

not first — first-language word order puts the negative early.

-s — the modal already carries the person; the main verb stays bare.

Speaking · run the toolbox talk (20')

Four cards, swap after each

CARD 1 Lead the talk — describe the near-miss, name the factor, recommend one fix.

CARD 2 Agree & add — agree, then add a fatigue or pressure example.

CARD 3 Speak up — name the unsafe norm politely but firmly.

CARD 4 Recommend — three measures, one modal verb in each.

Rule of the room: every recommendation carries a modal verb.

Writing · the toolbox-talk record

Write it in Simplified Technical English

90–120 words. Describe the near-miss. Name the human factor and its likely consequence. List two or three fixes with modals.

STE habits: short sentences · one idea per line · simple, approved words · active voice.

Wrap-up

Name the factor. Recommend the fix. Choose the modal.

should = advice  ·  must = a rule  ·  must not = a stop

Exit check: describe · add in the briefing · recommend with a modal
