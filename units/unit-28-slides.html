§0 · the situation

02:00. One aircraft. One failing pump. Four hours.

During the cruise, the green hydraulic system pressure dropped, then partly recovered. The aircraft is parked overnight and must fly again at 06:00.

Hydraulics move the primary flight controls. If a main pump has truly failed, this is not a small snag — it is a genuine showstopper. Tonight you find it, escalate it, and plan the recovery.

§0 · Interactive · what the crew saw

The green system pressure drop

Normal green pressure is 3000 psi. In the cruise it fell to about 1600, then partly recovered.

A partial recovery is not good news — it points to an intermittent leak, not a fixed fault. The BITE test and a visual check confirm the pump.

§1 · Listening 1 · Finding the snag

Marta & Deniss at the aircraft, 02:10

Marta: I have a serious snag on the number two hydraulic system.
Deniss: Let me run a BITE test and download the fault codes.
Deniss: If the pump is genuinely failing, this becomes a showstopper.

Listen for: how many fault codes? what do they point to? why can they not confirm the root cause yet?

Notice the habit: short sentences, one idea each, and the problem passed up the chain.

§1 · Listening 2 · Escalating to the supervisor

Deniss & Ivan, 02:35

Ivan: Can we defer it legally under the MEL and still operate?
Deniss: The green system pump is a definite no-go item.
Deniss: If we had ordered a spare earlier, we would have one now.

Listen for: no-go or deferrable? spare on site? the lead time? any safe workaround?

That last line is our grammar target — a mixed conditional. Hold it for §3.

§1 · Listening 3 · Briefing the manager (+ voicemail)

Ivan & Sandra (non-technical), 03:00

Sandra: I am not an engineer. Tell me what it means for the flight.
Ivan: A main hydraulic pump has failed. Hydraulics operate the primary flight controls.
Ivan: Around eight hours of downtime; return to service by noon.

The whole skill here is register: same facts, plainer words. Watch Ivan gloss every term for a non-engineer.

Numbers to catch: total downtime? update how often? (Every hour.)

Who calls whom — and why

Escalation is a controlled hand-up, not a panic. Each level is called only when the level below cannot decide or fix it.

§2 · Vocabulary · escalation words

Twenty terms — meaning + collocation

showstopper · escalate · workaround · AOG · MEL · defer · no-go item · snag · tech log · troubleshoot

BITE · AMM · IPC · lead time · spare · recovery plan · return to service · root cause · escalation · sign-off

Say them aloud: A-O-G, M-E-L, B-I-T-E, I-P-C as letters. Stress: NO-go item, SHOW-stopper.

Collocations to keep: run a BITE test · declare AOG · agree a recovery plan · find the root cause.

§2 · Interactive · technical vs plain

Same facts, two listeners

Adapting register is a graded B2 skill. The same defect is described one way to an engineer, another way to a manager.

Sort each line, then reword one technical sentence in plain English for the duty manager.

§3 · Grammar in context · mixed conditionals

Linking two different times in one sentence

PatternIf-clauseResultExample from tonight
Past → nowIf + had + past participlewould + base verbIf we had ordered it, we would have it now.
Now → pastIf + past simple (were)would have + past participleIf it were not a no-go item, we would have deferred it.

Both mix a past and a present in one sentence — that is why they are called mixed.

§3 · Interactive · build the conditional

Choose the correct half

Each item shows one half of a mixed conditional. Pick the correct other half.

The wrong options are the real learner traps — mostly "would" in the if-clause. Spot them and fix them.

§3 · the three L1 traps

Latvian & Russian: fix these three

If we would have ordered the part, we would have it now.
If we had ordered the part, we would have it now.
Pump is no-go item; we cannot defer defect.
The pump is a no-go item; we cannot defer the defect.
If the part was in stock, we fix it now.
If the part were in stock, we would fix it now.

§4 · Reading · real documentation genres

Tech log & AMM task card

Tech log (E5): defect → action → root cause → MEL → status → sign-off. It is the aircraft's running story.

AMM task card (E7): warning → preconditions → numbered steps → test → close-up. One instruction per step.

Genre habits worth naming: the warning comes first; every step is one action; the sign-off closes the job. This is exactly the Simplified Technical English you write in §6.

§4 · Interactive · the recovery plan

From AOG to return to service

A good recovery plan has a time and a next action. Here: order now, six hours of transport, two hours to fit and test, then sign-off.

Press run and watch the eight hours pass. Total downtime ≈ 8 h; return to service by noon.

§5 · managing the speaking

Coach with the B2 rubric — one focus each

Range · Accuracy · Fluency · Interaction · Coherence — score 1–5; B2 sits at 3–4.

Give one tip per pair: e.g. "add a connector — unless / as soon as", or "state the plan with a time".

B2 pass evidence: says no-go item → chooses to escalate → gives a lead time → agrees a recovery plan → adapts words for a non-engineer.

§6 · Writing · Simplified Technical English

Write it down and drive it to resolution

Task 1: a ~60-word tech log defect entry for the failed pump.

Task 2: a four-line escalation note to Maintenance Control — ask for the spare, state the recovery plan.

STE rules: one idea per sentence · under 18 words · approved plain words · the unit terms · "the" and "a" in place · end with a decision, not a description.

Recap · close

Snag → escalate → brief → recover → sign-off

Confirm the root cause · check the MEL (no-go, cannot defer) · declare AOG · escalate · get the lead time · brief the manager in plain words · agree a recovery plan · test and sign-off.
