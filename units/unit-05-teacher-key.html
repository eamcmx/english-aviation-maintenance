2 · First-language interference — Latvian & Russian

Latvian and Russian have no present perfect tense. Both mark whether an action is complete (aspect) rather than when it happened relative to now. So learners map the English present perfect onto their past tense and lose the done-and-relevant meaning that a handover depends on. The four errors below are predictable — name them so learners can self-monitor.

Error A — past simple where English needs the present perfect

✗ "I already signed the task card."  →  ✓ "I have already signed the task card."

Why: Latvian "es jau parakstīju" and Russian "я уже подписал" both use a single past form with "already" (jau / уже). There is no auxiliary to carry the "up-to-now" meaning, so learners produce a bare past simple. English forces have + participle with already, just, yet, so far.

Error B — present perfect with a finished past time

✗ "I have closed the defect at 14:00."  →  ✓ "I closed the defect at 14:00."

Why: Once learners do reach for the perfect, they over-apply it and attach a finished time. In their L1 the completed aspect can sit with any time word, so "at 14:00" feels compatible. In English a definite finished time (at 14:00, yesterday, at noon) blocks the perfect and requires the past simple.

Error C — dropped auxiliary "have" in questions and negatives

✗ "Did you finish the borescope yet?" / "The day team not started the job."  →  ✓ "Have you finished…?" / "…has not started."

Why: Neither L1 uses a "have" auxiliary to form questions or negatives, and Russian/Latvian negatives simply add a negative particle to the verb (nav / не). Learners therefore build "yet" questions with did or drop the auxiliary entirely. Drill the word order: Have/Has + subject + participle.

Error D — article & countability drift in the fixed terms

✗ "There is open defect." / "I raised work order."  →  ✓ "…an open defect." / "…a work order."

Why: Latvian and Russian have no articles. Learners omit a/an/the on the very terms they must use precisely. Because handover terms are countable ("a work order", "a deferred defect", "the tech log"), flag the missing article as a status-clarity issue, not just grammar.

3 · Extension activities (if time / for fast finishers)

  • Chinese-whispers handover. A briefs B on the three aircraft; B briefs C from memory; C reports back. Score for lost or wrong figures (times, MEL days, step numbers) — makes accuracy visible.
  • Defect-defer decision. Give three short defect cards (a cracked windscreen, a u/s cabin light, a leaking brake). Learners argue "safety-critical or MEL-deferrable?" in the target language, using because / unless / although.
  • Tense hunt. Learners scan a real (redacted) tech-log page and colour every present perfect green, every past simple amber, and justify one of each aloud.
  • STE rewrite race. Give a wordy, over-long handover note; pairs rewrite it into Simplified Technical English (short sentences, one idea per line). Shortest accurate version wins.
  • Avionics vs mechanics swap. Half the class handover the ATR radar job (avionics), half the A320 engine job (mechanics); then re-pair across trades so each explains the other's aircraft — stretches vocabulary both ways.
  • Live-caption dictation. Turn on the deck captions (CC / L), read a short briefing aloud, and have learners check the translation against their own note — builds listening for figures.

4 · CEFR B2 speaking rubric — the role-plays

Use during role-play cards 1–4. Five criteria, five bands. Band 4 = solid B2 (the target); band 3 = B1+ approaching; band 5 = B2+ secure. Give a mark per criterion, then a short comment.

Criterion 5 · B2+ 4 · B2 target 3 · B1+ 2 · B1
Range
vocabulary & structures		 Uses the full work-status field and documentation terms flexibly; varies structure naturally. Uses the taught terms correctly (deferred, outstanding, carried over, MEL, placard) with some variety. Uses core terms but repeats a few; limited structural range. Very few unit terms; leans on general words ("problem", "thing").
Accuracy
grammar, esp. tense		 Present perfect vs past simple consistently right; errors rare and self-corrected. Tense contrast mostly right; occasional slip that does not block meaning. Frequent tense errors (L1 traps A–C); meaning still mostly clear. Tense chosen almost at random; auxiliaries dropped; meaning often unclear.
Fluency Speaks smoothly at a natural handover pace; minimal hesitation. Keeps going with some pausing to plan; pace suits a briefing. Noticeable hesitation; needs prompting to continue. Halting; long pauses break the flow of the handover.
Interaction Asks and answers precise status questions; checks and confirms actively. Responds accurately to the incoming tech; asks relevant follow-ups. Answers when asked but rarely initiates a status question. Struggles to answer questions; little real exchange.
Coherence
connectors & order		 Aircraft-by-aircraft structure clear; connectors (because, although, unless) used well. Logical order; uses common connectors correctly. Some structure; connectors basic ("and", "so") or misused. Jumps between aircraft; hard to follow the status.

Band 1 (below B1) if the learner cannot sustain the exchange or convey the status of any aircraft. Priority feedback for this cohort: the tense contrast (Accuracy) and asking precise status questions (Interaction).
