02 Latvian & Russian interference — why each error happens

The grammar focus (comparatives for limits) attracts three predictable errors from Latvian and Russian first-language speakers. Name the cause, then recast — do not lecture during speaking.

1 · Double comparative — "more longer than"

Both Latvian and Russian mark the comparative with a single change — Latvian adds a prefix/ending (garš → garāks, "long → longer"), Russian adds an ending or uses более ("more") but never both at once. Learners meet English "more" and, unsure which pattern English uses, apply both the ending and "more" to be safe. Result: "more longer". Fix: English uses one or the other — short adjective takes -er only; long adjective takes more only. Never together.

2 · Wrong link word — "deeper as / from the limit"

Russian has no separate word for comparative "than": it uses the genitive case (глубже предела, literally "deeper of-the-limit") or чем. When learners reach for an English preposition, "from" leaks in from the genitive idea, and "as" leaks in from equality structures (as … as) and from other Baltic-region classroom English. Latvian uses nekā ("than"), which maps cleanly, but the "as" habit is contagious in mixed classes. Fix: the comparative link word in English is always than.

3 · "than" spelled/heard as "then" — "deeper then allowed"

English than and then differ by one vowel and sound almost identical in fast speech; neither Latvian nor Russian has this minimal pair, so the ear does not separate them. Learners write the more familiar word, "then". Fix: than = comparison (bigger than); then = time/sequence (first this, then that). Drill the contrast in E6.

Also watch (pronunciation/vocabulary): "corrosion" often loses the double r and the /ʒ/ sound; "scratch" is a hard consonant cluster for both L1s — drill it. Russian speakers may drop articles ("crack is on leading edge") — accept in speech, correct in the written report, because the report genre expects them.

03 Extension activities

  1. Limit-table race. Give pairs a mixed list of ten findings with dimensions and the AMM limits. First pair to label each within/out of limits with a correct comparative sentence wins. Targets speed + accuracy of the grammar under mild pressure.
  2. Borescope commentary. Show a still image or diagram of a blade with damage. One student describes the finding live in the four-part order (type, location, dimension, severity) while the partner writes the report line. Then swap and compare against a limit you supply.
  3. Avionics crossover. Give a BITE readout with a hard fault and an intermittent fault. Students decide, in English, which must be rectified before dispatch and which may be deferred — extending "accept / repair / refer" thinking into avionics for the mechanics-and-avionics mix.
  4. STE rewrite. Hand out a wordy, passive finding note ("It was observed that what appeared to be a crack was possibly present…"). Pairs rewrite it in Simplified Technical English: one idea per sentence, active voice, a comparative with than. Read the best aloud.
  5. Shift-handover chain. Round the room, each student adds one sentence to a spoken handover, keeping the report order and using at least one comparative. Trains fluency and the handover genre from Dialogue 4.

04 CEFR B2 speaking rubric — role-plays

Use for the four role-play cards. Score each pair on the five bands; give one strength and one target per pair. B2 is the target band; note where a learner reaches C1 or sits back at B1.

BandB1 (below)B2 (target)C1 (above)
Range Uses a few defect words; leans on one or two phrases; gaps force simpler ideas. Handles the unit's specialist terms and the report order; enough range to describe type, location, dimension, severity without groping. Flexible, precise vocabulary; varies phrasing; adds nuance (e.g. "the pitting is shallow but widespread").
Accuracy Comparatives often wrong (more longer, deeper as); errors block meaning at times. Comparatives generally correct with than; occasional slip self-corrected; errors rarely block meaning. Consistent control of comparatives and articles; errors are rare and minor.
Fluency Frequent pauses; report stalls between the four parts. Keeps going at a steady pace; some hesitation while planning the comparison, but the report flows. Smooth, near-effortless; pauses are for effect or precision, not for language.
Interaction Waits to be prompted; gives short answers; does not check the partner. Takes turns naturally; asks for the limit, confirms the decision, responds to the partner's finding. Leads and shapes the exchange; picks up the partner's point and builds a joint decision.
Coherence Parts come in a random order; reason for the decision missing or unclear. Follows the report order; links finding → limit → decision with so/because; the reason is clear. Well-organised, signposted; the justification is explicit and easy to follow.
Quick decision rule: a pair is at B2 if the comparison is done with a correct than-comparative and each decision carries a reason, even with a few self-corrected slips. Recast, do not stop the flow.
