2 · Latvian & Russian interference — why each error happens

Latvian and Russian have no continuous (progressive) tense. A single past form covers both "I worked" and "I was working". So learners must build a two-part system that does not exist in their first language, and three predictable errors follow.

Error 1 — past simple used for the background.
"When it slipped, I torqued the panel." → "…I was torquing the panel."
Why: in Latvian and Russian, aspect (finished vs ongoing) is carried by the verb's aspect pair or by context, not by a "was + -ing" structure. Learners map their single past form onto the English past simple and never reach for the continuous. Fix: teach the pair as one chunk — long background = was/were + -ing, short event = past simple — and drill "I was ___ing when it ___ed" until automatic.
Error 2 — was/were agreement.
"We was working under the flap." → "We were working…"
Why: Russian past-tense verbs agree with gender and number, not with a "be" auxiliary, and Latvian marks person on the main verb; neither language has an English-style be-auxiliary that changes was/were by subject. Learners over-generalise "was". Fix: a fixed rule card — I/he/she/it → was; we/you/they → were — plus quick substitution drills.
Error 3 — the dropped -ing.
"I was work on the panel." → "I was working…"
Why: the -ing form has no direct equivalent, so once learners produce "was" they revert to the bare verb they know. Fix: insist the two parts always travel together — "was" is never alone; it always brings an -ing. Choral repetition of the full form fixes it faster than a rule.

Two more to watch

  • Article dropping — "report to safety officer" → "to the safety officer". Neither first language uses articles, so learners omit them; flag gently in the report genre.
  • Present for past in reports — "When the tool fell, I am torquing." Learners slip into the present because the memory feels "live". Anchor the whole report firmly in the past.

3 · Extension activities (if time, or for fast finishers)

  • Occurrence-report relay. One student narrates a near-miss aloud in the past continuous; a partner writes it as a report in Simplified Technical English; a third checks it against the E5 model.
  • Fact/opinion hunt. Give a "bad" report full of blame words; pairs strike out every opinion and rewrite it as an observation.
  • Five-whys chain. Take a new hangar event (a slip, a spilled fluid, a wrong torque) and ask "why?" five times aloud until the class reaches a system-level root cause.
  • Toolbox talk. A student delivers a 60-second spoken safety brief modelled on the hangar announcement (Audio 4), using imperatives and short sentences.
  • Corrective vs preventive sort. Pairs write two of their own actions for a real workshop hazard — one corrective, one preventive — and defend the split.
  • Genre swap. Turn the E5 Occurrence Report into a one-line tech log entry, then into a task-card note — practising register compression.

5 · Full quiz key (quiz-rich.xml) — per-item rationale

Listening (7)

L1 First action after the wrench fell. Stopped the whole task at once. He makes the area safe before anything else; carrying on or picking the tool up keeps the hazard live.
L2 The wrench struck the aircraft. False. "It landed clear of the aircraft." Still a reportable near-miss with no damage.
L3 How many metres did it fall. 2. "dropped roughly two metres onto the concrete floor."
L4 Debrief in how many minutes. 10. "we will hold a proper debrief in ten minutes."
L5 Agreed root cause. Weak tool control. No lanyard, poor light and a shaky stand together — not one tired grip.
L6 Nobody was injured. True. "Nobody was injured, although it could have been very bad."
L7 Toolbox talk at what o'clock. 3. "a short toolbox talk at three o'clock."

Reading (5)

R1 Before stand S-14 is re-used. The loose rail must be repaired. Withdrawn and tagged; a work order for the rail repair is required first.
R2 Left in service for others. False. It was "withdrawn from service and tagged".
R3 Why the Service Bulletin issued. Operators reported dropped tools. The stated REASON line; the fix is tool lanyards.
R4 Adds a lanyard check to the task card. True. "Update the task card to add a lanyard check as Step 1."
R5 What to do after a dropped tool. Report it once the area is safe. Reportable even with no harm; do not wait until end of shift.

Use of English (7)

U1 I ___ (torque) … when it slipped. was torquing. Long background action → past continuous.
U2 We ___ (work) … were working. "we" takes "were".
U3 He ___ (not use) a lanyard. was not using / wasn't using. was + not + -ing.
U4 ___ you checking the tech log? Were. Question: be-form first; "you" takes "were".
U5 …while I ___ on the bolt. was working. "while" introduces the ongoing background.
U6 the root ___. cause. Root cause = the first, real reason.
U7 a ___ action (before a problem). preventive. Preventive acts before; corrective acts after.

Vocabulary (5)

V1 Term → meaning matching. near-miss = almost caused harm · hazard = could cause harm · contributing factor = helped cause it · observation = a fact you saw · mitigation = lowers a risk.
V2 Term → collocation. report a near-miss · find the root cause · agree a corrective action · clip a lanyard · hold a debrief.
V3 "Almost caused harm, but did not". Near-miss. Not a corrective action, sequence, or contributing factor.
V4 FOD = foreign object debris. True. A loose tool left behind becomes FOD.
V5 Fitting a lanyard after a drop. Corrective action. It stops a known problem happening again; preventive would act before any problem.

Anti-bias note: in every multiple-choice item the correct option is kept short and plain, with equal- or longer-length plausible distractors, so length gives no clue. Every item carries general feedback and every option carries its own feedback in the XML.
