Writing a defect report in Simplified Technical English.

A rambling shift note is not a defect report. In this unit you learn to rewrite messy notes into clear, safe, STE-compliant reports — approved words, one meaning per word, short sentences, and the articles English needs but your first language drops.

00The situation

It is the end of a late shift at the line station. You found a fault on the aircraft, and you scribbled a quick note before you went home. The note made sense to you at midnight, although it will not make sense to the next technician.

Tomorrow, the day shift must act on your words. If your report is vague, they waste time, or worse, they miss the danger. This is why maintenance organisations write in Simplified Technical English — a controlled language with approved words and short sentences, so that one report means one thing to everyone who reads it.

Your task in this unit is simple to say and hard to do well. You will take a rambling shift note and rewrite it, on your own, into an STE-compliant defect report. Then you will swap with a partner and peer-review each other's version, unless the instructor pairs you differently. Read the model dialogues first, and listen before you read.

Simplified Technical English was originally developed for the aerospace industry, precisely because ambiguous maintenance instructions were causing expensive and dangerous mistakes. The standard reduces the vocabulary deliberately, so that a single approved word carries a single meaning across every manual. For a technician whose first language is Latvian or Russian, this discipline is genuinely helpful, although the missing articles remain a persistent challenge in English.

Consider what happens when documentation fails at a busy line station. An instruction that a native speaker reads as obvious can appear genuinely ambiguous to a colleague from another linguistic background. In aviation, that single misunderstanding can propagate quietly into an incorrect installation or a missed inspection. Investigators repeatedly identify unclear procedures as a contributing factor in maintenance errors, alongside fatigue and interruption. By constraining vocabulary and sentence length together, Simplified Technical English attacks the linguistic component of that risk directly, which explains its wide adoption across manufacturers and regulators.

Dialogue 1 — From shift note to defect report
Transcript

AToms, I read your shift note from last night. It is hard to follow.

BI wrote it fast at the end of the shift. What is wrong with it?

AA defect report is not a shift note. It is a formal record, so it must use Simplified Technical English.

BSo I cannot write it the way I speak?

ANo. Every word must be an approved word with one meaning. That removes ambiguity.

BShow me. My note says the part near the door looked bad.

AThat is the problem. Did you find corrosion, a crack, or a loose bolt?

BThere was corrosion on the bracket below the panel.

AGood. Now write it as a fact. The bracket has corrosion. Short and clear.

BThat does sound better than my note.

AThe tech log needs the same clear style, because the next technician depends on it. Put one idea in each short sentence.

BMy note also says it seemed like it might be leaking. Is that wrong?

AYes. Seemed and might give ambiguity. Write what you saw, not what you guessed.

BSo I should write that fuel is on the floor below the bracket?

AExactly. That is a fact an inspector can check. That is a defect report.

BI understand now. Short sentences, approved words, one meaning.

Dialogue 2 — Write the procedure in STE
Transcript

AYour report is clear. But the repair steps are not in Simplified Technical English yet.

BWhat should the steps look like?

AEach step is a procedural sentence. It is a command in the active voice.

BCan you give me an example?

AStart with a technical verb. Write: Remove the bolt from the bracket.

BMy draft says the removal of the bolt should be done. That is heavy.

AIt is heavy and passive. Use the active voice instead, and put one clear instruction in each line.

BYou also marked wing attachment fitting bolt hole. Why?

AThat is a long noun cluster. Three nouns together create ambiguity.

BHow do I fix a noun cluster?

AUse the technical name and small words. Write: the bolt hole in the fitting.

BThat is easier to read. What about danger notes?

APut a warning before the step when a person can be hurt.

BAnd when only the part can be damaged?

AThen you write a caution. A warning is for people, a caution is for the part.

BSo every approved word has one meaning, and each instruction stays short.

Dialogue 3 — Peer review before sign-off
Transcript

ABefore I sign the work order, let us peer-review your defect report together.

BI made the sentences short and used approved words.

AGood. But here you dropped an article. You wrote: Remove bolt from bracket.

BIn my language we do not use articles, so I forget them.

AIn English they are not optional. Write: Remove the bolt from the bracket.

BI see. So every noun needs the or a.

AUsually, yes. You write a crack the first time you mention it, and then the crack after that.

BHere I wrote that the part is okay. Is that an approved word?

ANo. Write serviceable if it is good, or unserviceable if it is not.

BThe bracket has corrosion, so it is unserviceable.

ACorrect, so tag it now. Then add the caution about the sealant near the panel, unless it is already there.

BShould the warning about the live circuit go first?

AYes. The warning protects the person, so it comes before the step.

BI will update the tech log with the same words.

AGood. Now the report is clear, and I can sign the work order.

BThank you. Next time I will add the articles before you ask.

Dialogue 4 — Line supervisor voicemail (monologue)
Transcript

Hi, this is the line supervisor with a short message for the late shift. Please listen to the whole note.

We have an important new rule for every defect report. From today, without exception, all reports must use Simplified Technical English.

Do not copy your rough shift note straight into the tech log. Rewrite it first.

Use approved words, and give each word one meaning. Keep every sentence short and clear.

If you find corrosion on a bracket, write it as a plain fact. Do not guess.

Add a warning before any step that can hurt a person. Add a caution when only the part can be damaged.

When the report is ready, attach it to the work order and call me. Thank you, and have a safe shift.

03Grammar in context — obligatory articles

STE keeps sentences short, so every word must earn its place. Even so, English still needs its articles. You cannot drop the or a, although Latvian and Russian have no articles at all. This is the error the instructor will spot first.

Look again at two lines from the dialogues. In Dialogue 2, the mentor gives the model step:

"Start with a technical verb. Write: Remove the bolt from the bracket."

Every noun has an article. It is the bolt and the bracket, because the reader already knows which ones. In Dialogue 3, the supervisor corrects a dropped article:

"You dropped an article. You wrote: Remove bolt from bracket."

The naked nouns feel normal in Latvian or Russian, yet in an English report they read as broken. The difficulty is understandable, because neither language marks a noun as definite or indefinite with a separate word. Consequently, the article is often the very last feature a fluent speaker masters, unless they practise it deliberately in their own writing. Use the table below to choose the right article, and then check every noun in your defect report against it.

CaseRuleSTE example
Known thingUse the when the reader knows which one.Remove the bolt from the bracket.
First mentionUse a / an the first time; use the after that.Find a crack. Measure the crack.
One of manyUse a / an for any one, not a special one.Install a new seal.
General / uncountableUse no article for a material or a plural in general.Check for corrosion. Torque all bolts.

Why the article matters for safety

An article is not decoration; in a maintenance instruction it identifies exactly which component the technician must handle. Whenever you write "the bracket", you are signalling that a particular, already-mentioned bracket is meant, rather than any similar component nearby. When you drop the article, you quietly reintroduce the very ambiguity that Simplified Technical English exists to eliminate. Therefore, experienced reviewers treat every missing article as a genuine documentation defect, comparable to an omitted torque value.

Articles also interact with countability in ways that reward a little study. A singular countable noun, such as "bolt" or "bracket", almost always needs an article or another determiner in front of it. An uncountable noun, such as "corrosion" or "fuel", takes no article when you mean the substance in general, although it takes "the" once you specify a particular quantity. Recognising this distinction quickly will eliminate the majority of the article errors that currently appear in your reports.

Wrong ✗ — Technician found crack in panel and replaced bracket.
Right ✓ — The technician found a crack in the panel and replaced the bracket.
Wrong ✗ — Fault is in left hydraulic system.
Right ✓ — The fault is in the left hydraulic system.
Wrong ✗ — Remove bolt, then install new seal on valve.
Right ✓ — Remove the bolt, then install a new seal on the valve.

E5 · Reading 1

Read a completed defect report

Tech Log — Defect Report · A/C reg YL-BAM · ATA 32 Landing Gear

Defect: During the walk-around check, the technician found corrosion on the left main gear bracket. The corrosion is on the lower face of the bracket, near the bolt hole. The area is about two centimetres wide. There is no crack in the bracket, and no fluid is present.

Action: The technician cleaned the surface and measured the depth of the corrosion. The depth is within the limit in the maintenance manual, although it is close to the limit. The technician tagged the bracket as serviceable for now. A repeat check is due at the next A-check, unless the depth grows.

Warning: Do not apply load to the gear before the area is clean and dry. Caution: Do not use a steel brush on the surface, because it can damage the coating. The work order stays open until the repeat check is complete.

Note for the reviewer: the original technician recorded the observation immediately, rather than relying on memory at the end of the shift. Consequently, the measurements remain precise, and a second engineer can repeat the inspection and reach an identical conclusion. This traceability is precisely what distinguishes a professional defect report from a hurried personal note.

Read a Service Bulletin summary

Service Bulletin SB-737-27-1145 · Rudder PCU Bracket · Summary for line stations

Reason: The manufacturer has received reports of corrosion on the bracket that holds the rudder power control unit. In a small number of aircraft, the corrosion reduces the strength of the bracket over time. If the bracket becomes unserviceable, the rudder control can be affected.

Action required: At the next scheduled check, inspect the bracket for corrosion and cracks. Use the technical name of each part in the defect report. Write every step as a procedural sentence in Simplified Technical English, so that the record has no ambiguity for the next technician.

Compliance: This bulletin is recommended, unless your authority makes it mandatory. Record the inspection in the tech log and close the work order only when the bracket is confirmed serviceable. A warning applies during the check: support the rudder before you remove any bracket bolt, because the surface can move without warning.

Additional guidance: line stations should treat this recommendation seriously even before it becomes mandatory, because corrosion progresses gradually and often unpredictably. Where an aircraft operates near the coast, atmospheric humidity accelerates the deterioration, and the interval between inspections may therefore need shortening. Record any such decision, together with its justification, so that the reasoning remains visible to every subsequent technician.

05Speaking — role-play

Work in pairs. Take a card each. Use the useful phrases. Swap roles after five minutes.

Speaking about a defect out loud is surprisingly different from writing one, because conversation constantly tempts you back into vague, everyday vocabulary. During each role-play, discipline yourself to describe the fault using the very same approved words that your written report would contain. If your partner hears any ambiguity, they should interrupt immediately, so that you practise correcting yourself under mild but realistic professional pressure.

06Writing task

Now do the unit task on your own. Below is a rambling shift note. Rewrite it as a clean defect report in Simplified Technical English. Then use the self-check list, and peer-review a partner's version.

Professional maintenance writing is judged not by elegance but by whether an independent inspector can act on it without asking a single clarifying question. As you rewrite the note below, imagine that particular reader precisely, and remove anything that would force them to guess your intention. A report that survives that imagined inspection, with every article present and every word carrying exactly one meaning, meets the standard this entire unit has been training you towards.

Rough shift note (rewrite this)

"So basically when I was doing the walk-round on the right side I noticed the little metal thing that holds the antenna, it looked kind of rough near the bottom, might be some corrosion or maybe just dirt, hard to tell in the dark, anyway I wiped it and it still looked bad so probably someone should look at it, also there was a bolt that felt a bit loose but I didn't have the torque wrench so I left it, tell the day shift."

Write six to eight short lines. Use a defect line, an action line, a warning or caution, and the state of the part (serviceable or unserviceable).

Model answer (open after you write)

Defect: There is corrosion on the antenna bracket on the right side. The corrosion is near the lower edge, close to the bolt hole.

Action: The technician cleaned the surface. The corrosion is still visible. The technician did not torque the loose bolt, because the torque wrench was not available.

State: The bracket is unserviceable until an inspector checks it.

Caution: Do not apply load to the antenna before the bolt is torqued to the correct value. Record this in the tech log and keep the work order open.
