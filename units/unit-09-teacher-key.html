2 · Latvian and Russian interference — the errors and why they happen

Both first languages are here. The three patterns below are the ones to pre-empt and to recast during monitoring.

2.1 Future tense in the if-clause

✗ If it will fail the bench test, we replace it.   ✓ If it fails the bench test, we replace it.

Why: Latvian and Russian mark the future in both clauses of a conditional (Latvian ja tas neizturēs…, Russian если он не пройдёт… use a future/perfective form after "if"). English does not: the condition stays in the present, and only the result can carry will. Learners transfer the home-language pattern and add will after if. Recast by echoing the correct form: "yes — if it fails."

2.2 Dropped articles

✗ If breaker trips, technician resets it.   ✓ If the breaker trips, the technician resets it.

Why: Neither Latvian nor Russian has articles. There is no a/the to transfer, so learners simply omit them — most often before a known, specific part (the breaker, the harness, the actuator). Flag it as a "missing word" rather than a grammar rule; a quick "which breaker?" prompts the the.

2.3 Future after when / as soon as

✗ When you will find a short circuit, tell me.   ✓ As soon as you find a short circuit, tell me.

Why: the same transfer as 2.1, extended to time clauses. In Latvian and Russian a future action after "when" takes a future form; English keeps the present in the time clause. Teach when, as soon as, unless and in case together as "present-tense triggers".

2.4 Two smaller points to monitor

  • "replace to" — Russian/Latvian verbs of substitution take a preposition; English replace is transitive. Model replace the actuator (no to).
  • Word stress — INtermittent, conTINuity, SERviceable. First-language stress patterns pull the stress the wrong way; drill the marked syllable.

3 · Extension activities (for fast finishers or a follow-up class)

  • Fault-isolation relay. One learner names a symptom; the next proposes the first test with if; the third states the result and the next test. Keep the chain going for six steps. Builds the sequencing can-do at speed.
  • Disagree politely. Give one learner a firm (wrong) diagnosis to defend and the partner the job of disagreeing with reasons. Target phrases: "I see your point, but…", "I am not sure that is the root cause", "Can we test the wiring first?"
  • Document swap. Learners rewrite the E5 AMM task as a short tech-log entry in Simplified Technical English, then compare with the writing model. Focus on one instruction per sentence.
  • BITE code hunt. Give three invented BITE readouts with fault codes and short symptoms. Learners decide, for each, whether to swap the unit or test the wiring first, and justify with if.
  • Defer or rectify debate. A four-line MEL extract plus "no part in stock". Pairs argue defer vs. ground the aircraft, then agree and record the decision in the tech log.
