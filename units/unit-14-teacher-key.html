02Latvian & Russian interference — why each error happens

These are the predictable errors for this group. Naming the cause helps students self-correct rather than memorise a list.

1 · The extra "to" — must to be done → must be done

Both Latvian (jābūt izdarītam) and Russian (должно быть сделано) express obligation with a construction that behaves like an infinitive. Learners map that onto English and insert the infinitive marker to after the modal. English modals must/shall take the bare base verb. Drill: "must + verb, no to" — chorus five clean examples.

2 · The dropped "be" — must done → must be done

Neither L1 builds the English passive with a separate auxiliary the way English does (must + be + past participle). Russian forms the passive with a single participle/verb ending; Latvian similarly. So the auxiliary be disappears. Drill: show the three-part chain visually — MUST · BE · DONE — and have them tap three fingers.

3 · must not for need not (no obligation)

Russian не должен and Latvian nedrīkst / nav jā- blur the line English draws sharply: must not = a prohibition (banned), need not = no obligation (permitted, but not required). Learners reach for must not as the "natural" negative of must. Here it is safety-relevant: after the terminating action the repeat is not banned — it is simply no longer required. Drill: a two-column sort of sentences into BAN vs NO-OBLIGATION.

Bonus · dropped articles — is corrective action → is the corrective action

Neither Latvian nor Russian has articles, so a/an/the are routinely dropped or over-used. Flag it lightly during monitoring; do not let it derail the modal focus.

Pronunciation watch

Stress the numbers said as words — "fifteen hundred" vs "fifty", "thirty" vs "thirteen". Russian/Latvian speakers may flatten the vowel in cycle (/ˈsaɪkəl/) and voice the final consonant in date. These matter because the voicemail and the numerical quiz items test spoken figures.

03Extension activities (fast finishers / a second lesson)

  • AD hunt. Give each pair a short real EASA AD summary. They extract action, applicability, threshold, interval and deadline into a one-line planning note. Compare across pairs.
  • Genre swap. Turn the AD extract (E5) into a spoken voicemail like Dialogue 4, or turn the voicemail into a written work-order line. Tests register control.
  • Whichever-comes-first maths. Give three aircraft different flights-per-day figures; students compute which limit (500 FC or 90 days) bites first and the real deadline. Mirrors the deck clock widget.
  • Devil's advocate planner. One student is a planner who keeps finding reasons to delay; the engineer must keep re-stating the obligation with strong modals until the slot holds. Great for fluency and modal recycling.
  • STE re-write. Hand out a deliberately wordy briefing note; students rewrite it in Simplified Technical English (short lines, one idea, consistent term for the pin).
  • Error clinic. Collect the modal errors you noted while monitoring and run an anonymous board correction at the start of the next lesson.

04CEFR B2 speaking rubric — the role-plays

Score each learner across five criteria. B2 is the target band; the descriptors below sit one band lighter (approaching B2) and one heavier (secure B2 / into C1) so you can place borderline students.

CriterionApproaching (B1+)Target — B2Secure B2 → C1
RangeRelies on a few memorised phrases; limited bank terms; repeats "must" for everything.Uses the bank terms accurately and varies modals (must, shall, must not, need not) to fit meaning.Flexible, precise vocabulary; recasts and paraphrases when a word is missing; natural collocations ("meet the deadline").
AccuracyFrequent modal errors (extra to, missing be) that sometimes obscure meaning.Good control of must/shall forms; errors occur but rarely block meaning; self-corrects some.High accuracy; the passive obligation and the must not/need not contrast are reliably correct.
FluencyFrequent pauses; the briefing stalls; needs prompting.Speaks at a fairly even pace; some hesitation while planning language, but keeps the briefing moving.Smooth, near-effortless; hesitation is for content, not language.
InteractionWaits to be asked; little turn-taking; does not respond to push-back.Initiates and responds; answers the planner's questions; handles push-back on the slot with a modal.Manages the exchange — checks understanding, concedes or defends the deadline, closes the deal.
CoherenceFacts in random order; the planner must piece it together.Logical order — action, date, deadline, risk — linked with because / so / unless.Well-structured briefing; signposts clearly; connectors used for real emphasis, not filler.

Quick pass mark: a learner is "at B2" for this task if they deliver the action + deadline + grounding risk in a logical order, using at least three different modal forms correctly, without breakdown.

Marking notes

  • Short-answer items are case-insensitive. Accept the listed variants only; a bare "deadline" for U6 earns half marks by design.
  • Numerical items (L4, L5) have zero tolerance — the figure must be exact. Wrong-but-plausible figures (500 for the interval; 90 for the threshold) carry targeted feedback.
  • Every question has general feedback; every option has its own feedback, so the quiz teaches when students review it.
  • Pass mark suggestion: 70%. Below that, the most common gaps are the threshold/interval mix-up (L4, U7) and the must not/need not contrast (U2) — reteach those two points.
