2 · First-language interference — Latvian & Russian

Both languages have no articles and mark definiteness in other ways. This produces predictable, correctable errors. Anticipate them; frame each as a documentation issue, not a personal failing.

Error A · Dropping the and a entirely

✗ Remove bolt from bracket.  →  ✓ Remove the bolt from the bracket.

Why it happens: Latvian and Russian have no separate word for the/a; definiteness is carried by word order, case, or context. So the article is inaudible to the learner's ear — it is not carelessness, it is a category their L1 simply does not encode. The article is often the last feature a fluent speaker masters.

How to fix: teach a checking habit, not a rule to feel. After writing, the learner points at every singular noun and asks "the, a, or none?" Treat a missing article as a defect, like a missing torque value.

Error B · Over-using the with general / uncountable nouns

✗ Check for the corrosion.  →  ✓ Check for corrosion.

Why it happens: once learners "discover" articles, they over-correct and insert the everywhere, including before general materials and plurals. Russian speakers also map the onto the demonstrative "this/that", so a general substance wrongly becomes specific.

How to fix: contrast the substance in general (corrosion, no article) with a specific, known patch (the corrosion on the lower face). Use the countable/uncountable line from the grammar section.

Error C · Word-for-word calques and heavy noun phrases

✗ The removal of the bolt should be done.  →  ✓ Remove the bolt.

Why it happens: Russian and Latvian technical registers favour nominalisation ("the removal of…") and impersonal/passive constructions, which learners transfer directly into English. The result is grammatical but heavy and un-STE.

How to fix: insist on a technical verb first, active voice, one instruction per line. Model the transformation aloud several times so the pattern becomes automatic.

Extra watch-points

  • False friends / register: learners may reach for "okay", "bad", "fine". Redirect to the approved pair serviceable / unserviceable.
  • Pronunciation: corrosion (/kəˈrəʊʒən/) and serviceable (four syllables) are commonly mis-stressed; drill them.
  • /w/ vs /v/: both L1 groups may swap these — "warning" can sound like "varning". Note it, do not over-correct in speaking.

3 · Extension activities

  • Real-document rewrite: bring a genuine (anonymised) tech-log entry and have stronger pairs convert it to STE, then justify each change.
  • Ambiguity hunt: pairs write one deliberately ambiguous line each; the class rewrites it with one meaning. Fast finishers extend it into a full defect report.
  • Article dictation: read ten short STE steps at speaking pace; learners write only the articles they hear (the / a / —). Self-mark against the grammar table.
  • Warning vs caution sort: give twelve mixed hazard cards; teams sort into warning (people) / caution (part) and place each correctly before or after its step.
  • AI conversation partner: point pairs at the chat-buddy prompts in the body page to rehearse a peer review at B2 with gentle correction.
  • Cross-trade swap: a mechanic and an avionics learner each describe a fault in their own field; the partner must reach the same understanding using only the report.

4 · CEFR B2 speaking rubric — role-plays

Use for the four role-play cards. Five criteria, four bands. B2 is the target band; below it is B1, above it is C1. Score each criterion, then give one improvement point per learner.

CriterionBelow B2 (B1)B2 — targetAbove B2 (C1)
Range
vocabulary		 Everyday words; reaches for "okay/bad"; avoids the unit terms. Uses the approved unit terms correctly (serviceable, warning, corrosion, procedural sentence) with enough range to describe a fault clearly. Precise, flexible use of specialist terms; rephrases smoothly when a word is missing.
Accuracy
incl. articles		 Frequent dropped/added articles; errors sometimes block meaning. Good control; articles mostly correct; occasional slips that do not block meaning. Consistent accuracy, including articles and countability; errors rare.
Fluency Short, hesitant turns; long pauses to search for words. Speaks at a fairly even pace; pauses to plan but keeps the exchange going. Smooth, near-effortless delivery even on the technical content.
Interaction Waits to be asked; rarely initiates or checks. Takes turns well; interrupts appropriately to flag ambiguity or a missing article; asks for/gives clarification. Manages the exchange, steers the review, repairs breakdowns effortlessly.
Coherence Ideas listed without links; hard to follow the sequence. Uses connectors (because, unless, before/after) to order the defect, action, warning and state clearly. Well-structured argument; clear signposting of the whole report logic.

Quick pass mark: a learner meets the unit's speaking outcome at B2 when they can describe a defect out loud using approved words with correct articles, and can interrupt a partner to flag one dropped article or one ambiguity.
