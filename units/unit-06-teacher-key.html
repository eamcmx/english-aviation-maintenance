2 · First-language interference (Latvian and Russian)

All three modal errors below are predictable transfers. Name the pattern, not just the wrong word, so learners can self-correct.

We must to check the torque.   We must check the torque.

Why it happens: both Latvian (mums jāpārbauda) and Russian (мы должны проверить) join a second verb with an infinitive marker or a debitive form. Learners map that onto English and insert "to". Fix: most modals take the bare base verb; only ought keeps "to". Drill "must check, should check, can check" as a fixed frame.

You not should take a call.   You should not take a call.

Why it happens: Russian negates by placing не directly before the verb (не должен), and Latvian ne- attaches to the verb too. The negative therefore lands before the modal. Fix: in English "not" comes after the modal (should not / must not / cannot). Contrast the two orders on the board and drill the contraction "shouldn't".

He should checks the lockwire.   He should check the lockwire.

Why it happens: Latvian and Russian mark person and number on the main verb, so learners carry agreement onto "check" after a modal. English modals are invariable and already carry the person, so the main verb stays bare. Fix: "he should check / she must file / it can fail" — no "-s" after a modal.

Two more to watch

  • Article drop. Neither first language has articles, so expect "file incident report" for "file an incident report". Recast, do not lecture.
  • "Make" vs "do" a check. Russian сделать проверку transfers as "make a cross-check". The English collocation is "do a cross-check / carry out a check".

3 · Extension activities (for fast finishers or a second lesson)

  • Real toolbox talk (5 min). Each learner leads a 90-second talk on a different Dirty Dozen factor (stress, teamwork, resources) not covered in class. Peers add one point using an agree-and-add phrase.
  • Rewrite in STE. Give a wordy paragraph from a maintenance manual; learners rewrite it as short one-idea sentences with approved words, then compare.
  • Incident-report chain. In threes, one describes a near-miss, one asks the six report questions (what, where, when, who, why, what next), one writes the report. Rotate.
  • Modal auction. Read sentences with a modal gap; teams bid on the strength (advice / rule / prohibition) and justify. Wrong strength loses the points.
  • Chat-partner drill. Learners run a two-minute talk with the on-page partner, then report one correction it gave them.

4 · CEFR B2 speaking rubric — role-plays

Score each learner 1–5 on the five criteria during the role-play. Band 5 = strong B2 (approaching C1); band 3 = solid B2; band 1 = below B2 (A2–B1). Bands 4 and 2 sit between.

CriterionBand 5 — strong B2Band 3 — B2Band 1 — below B2
RangeUses the unit terms and a variety of modals precisely; varied phrasing.Uses most unit terms and 2–3 modals; some repetition.Very limited words; one or two memorised phrases only.
AccuracyModals consistently correct (base verb, "not" placed right); errors rare and self-repaired.Mostly correct modals; the L1 errors appear but do not block meaning.Frequent modal errors ("must to", "-s", "not should") that obscure meaning.
FluencySpeaks at a natural pace with short, well-linked sentences; few pauses.Keeps going with some pausing to plan; short sentences.Halting; long pauses; relies on the first language.
InteractionAgrees, adds and invites the partner in; genuine turn-taking in the briefing register.Agrees and adds a point; responds to the partner but rarely invites.Waits to be prompted; little or no agree-and-add.
CoherenceClear order: names the factor, the consequence, then the fix; connectors used well.Covers factor and fix; order mostly clear; basic connectors.Points listed without order; consequence or fix missing.

Pass mark for the outcome = band 3 or above on Accuracy and Interaction, since those carry the grammar focus and the "agree and add" can-do. Give each pair one strong line and one modal error as feedback.
