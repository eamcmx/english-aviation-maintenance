01The situation

A pilot has just landed. She says the brakes "feel wrong," but she cannot say more. Your job is to turn that vague snag into a clear, precise defect entry in the tech log.

The tech log is the legal record of the aircraft's condition. Because the next crew trusts your words, every entry must be exact. A vague report helps nobody, so you question the pilot for the real detail. You ask what the symptom is, where it happens, and under which condition it appears. Then you write only what you can support, and you keep any guess out of the record.

This is a language skill as much as a technical one. You rephrase a loose complaint into the precise terms of the trade. You also keep a hard line between an observation and an assumption, because the two are not the same. An observation is a fact you have seen with your own eyes. An assumption is a guess about the cause, and a guess does not belong in a defect entry. Learn this discipline well, because a clear entry saves the next shift hours of work.

Consider the professional consequences of an imprecise entry. An inaccurate description delays the diagnosis, because the next technician cannot reproduce the condition. Consequently, the investigation consumes additional labour, although the original defect remains completely unchanged. A disciplined description therefore protects the operation, the schedule, and ultimately the passengers. Whenever the terminology feels difficult, remember that precision communicates responsibility to every colleague.

Experienced engineers treat documentation as a genuine safety instrument, not administrative decoration. A carefully worded observation preserves information that memory inevitably loses overnight. Furthermore, an unambiguous entry survives interpretation between international maintenance organisations. Because aviation operates continuously, your description must communicate independently, without any verbal explanation. This professional discipline distinguishes a competent technician from a merely enthusiastic amateur.

A good defect entry answers four questions

  • What is the symptom? Describe the sign you can actually observe.
  • Where is it? Give the exact location on the aircraft.
  • When does it happen? State the condition, such as during landing.
  • Is it fact or guess? Record the observation, and leave out the assumption.

02Listen: three defect reports

Listen first, and do not read the transcript yet. Afterwards, listen a second time and open the transcript to check what you understood.

Dialogue 1 — Turning the pilot's snag into a tech-log entry

Two mechanics read a vague pilot report and question it before they write anything.
Transcript

AThe pilot has raised a snag on the brakes. The pilot report is very vague.

BWhat exactly does the pilot report say about the brakes?

AIt only says 'something feels wrong with the brakes.' That is not a clear symptom.

BWe cannot write that in the tech log. We need the real symptom and the location.

AAgreed. So let us question the pilot before we record the defect.

BGood idea. Ask when the problem happens, because the condition matters a lot.

AThe pilot says the pedal feels soft during a normal landing. That is a useful symptom.

BSo the observation is a soft pedal during landing. We have a clear symptom now.

ACareful. 'The seal has failed' is only an assumption, not an observation.

BYou are right. We record what we have seen, not what we guess.

ASo the entry needs the symptom, the location, and the condition.

BExactly. The location is the left main gear, and the condition is during landing.

AI will write it now. 'The left brake pedal has felt soft during landing.'

BThat is precise. It gives the fact, and it avoids any assumption about the cause.

AGood. I have entered the defect in the tech log for the next shift.

BWell done. Now the inspection can start from a clear, honest report.

Observation or assumption?

Write only what you can see or measure. "There is a leak at the union" is an observation. "The seal has failed" is an assumption about the cause. The inspection will test the cause later, so keep guesses out of the entry. When you are unsure, describe the symptom and its location, and let the next mechanic find the cause.

Dialogue 2 — Describing the defect precisely

The mechanics inspect the brake unit and describe each defect in exact terms.
Transcript

AI have inspected the left brake unit. I have found a hydraulic leak.

BWhere exactly is the leak located? The location must be precise.

AThe leak is at the brake line union, near the wheel. Fluid has pooled below it.

BUnderstood. Is the line damaged, or is only the fitting loose?

AThe line shows chafing. It has rubbed against a bracket for some time.

BChafing is serious, because the rubbing can wear through the line.

AI agree. There is also light corrosion on the bracket surface.

BHas the corrosion caused a crack, or is the metal still sound?

AI can see no crack by eye. I will use the borescope to check inside.

BGood. Check the wheel bearing too, because you mentioned some play.

AYes, the bearing has developed play. There is clear wear on the race.

BSo we have three findings: a leak, chafing, and bearing play.

ACorrect. Each is an observation, and I have recorded the location of each.

BThe symptom the pilot felt now makes sense. The soft pedal came from the leak.

AI have written every defect in the tech log. The unit is unserviceable until repair.

BAgreed. Do not release the aircraft until we rectify all three defects.

Dialogue 3 — Avionics: an intermittent fault and the BITE readout

Two avionics technicians chase a fault that comes and goes, and record it honestly.
Transcript

AThe pilot report says the display goes blank in flight. The fault is intermittent.

BAn intermittent fault is hard to find, because it comes and goes.

AExactly. On the ground the display works, so we cannot see the symptom now.

BLet us run the BITE. The self-test may have stored a fault code.

AGood plan. I have started the BITE readout on the avionics unit.

BWhat does the readout show? Read the code and the message aloud.

AThe BITE shows a fault on the data bus. It logged it during the last flight.

BSo we have a real observation now, not just the pilot report.

ACareful. 'The unit has failed' is still an assumption at this stage.

BTrue. The cause could be a loose plug, and the condition is vibration in flight.

AI will inspect the connectors for chafing and corrosion first.

BGood. Note the exact location of any damage, so the next shift can rectify it.

AOne connector shows chafing on the loom. I have found the likely cause.

BRecord it as an observation. The BITE code and the chafing agree well.

AI have entered the defect in the tech log. The system is unserviceable for now.

BAgreed. We cannot sign it off until the fault is cleared and tested.

04Grammar in context: the present perfect

A defect you found is still there now, so you use the present perfect. It links a past action to the present result. This is the natural tense for a live, unrepaired fault.

Look at these lines from the dialogues, because each links the past to now:

"The panel has developed cracks." · "I have found a hydraulic leak." · "The bearing has developed play."

Each sentence uses has or have plus the past participle. The action happened in the past, but the result is true now. So the crack is still there, the leak is still there, and the play is still there. You choose this tense because the defect still matters. For a finished, closed job you would use the past simple instead.

This particular distinction frequently confuses international learners, because their languages express completion quite differently. Nevertheless, the present perfect communicates a continuing, unresolved consequence, which maintenance documentation absolutely requires. A corroded bracket that has already cracked remains dangerous until somebody rectifies it. Consequently, technicians instinctively prefer this construction whenever a discovered defect still affects airworthiness. Practise the participle forms deliberately, until the grammatical pattern feels completely automatic.

The form

PurposeFormExample
Positivehas / have + past participleThe panel has developed cracks.
Negativehas / have not + past participleThe technician has not rectified the leak.
QuestionHas / Have + subject + past participleHave you found the leak yet?
With for / sincehas / have + participle + for / sinceThe valve has leaked for two flights.

Three mistakes to avoid

Latvian and Russian have no present perfect tense. So speakers of both languages often use the past simple or the present simple instead. Study each correction with care.

WrongRightWhy
The line leaks since yesterday.The line has leaked since yesterday.A fault that started before and lasts now needs the present perfect.
The panel has develop cracks.The panel has developed cracks.After has or have, use the past participle, not the base verb.
The valve has leaked since two flights.The valve has leaked for two flights.Use for with a length of time, and since with a start point.

One more habit matters for aviation English. Your defect entry should use Simplified Technical English, the controlled writing standard of maintenance documents. It prefers one idea per sentence, an approved verb, and no extra words. So write "The bearing has developed play," and stop. Keep every line short, exact, and easy to read under pressure.

06Speaking: role-play

Work with a partner and use the useful phrases. Question a vague report, describe a defect, and keep fact apart from guess.

Swap roles after each card, so both of you practise asking and describing. Speak slowly and clearly, exactly as you would in a noisy hangar where a misheard word can cause a real defect.

Card 1 — Question the pilot report

Situation: A pilot has raised a vague snag: "the brakes feel wrong."

Your role: You are the line mechanic who receives the report.

Your goal: Ask for the symptom, the location, and the condition, until the report is clear.

Useful phrases: "What exactly do you feel?" · "When does it happen?" · "So the symptom is a soft pedal during landing."

Card 2 — Describe the defect you found

Situation: You have inspected the brake unit and found several defects.

Your role: You report your findings to the certifying mechanic.

Your goal: Describe the leak, the chafing, and the play, and give the exact location of each.

Useful phrases: "I have found a leak at the union." · "The line shows chafing." · "The bearing has developed play."

Card 3 — Fact or assumption?

Situation: A trainee wants to write "the seal has failed" in the tech log.

Your role: You are the experienced mechanic who checks the entry.

Your goal: Explain the difference between an observation and an assumption, with one example of each.

Useful phrases: "That is an assumption, not an observation." · "Write what you saw." · "Describe the symptom and the location."

Card 4 — An intermittent avionics fault

Situation: A display goes blank only in flight, and the fault is intermittent.

Your role: You are the avionics technician on the line.

Your goal: Explain the symptom, run the BITE, and say why the unit is unserviceable for now.

Useful phrases: "The fault is intermittent." · "The BITE has stored a code." · "The unit is unserviceable until we test it."

07Writing: a tech-log defect entry

Write a short defect entry for the tech log. Use Simplified Technical English: short sentences, one idea each, and the present perfect for the live fault.

Task: A pilot reports that the number two engine oil pressure "looks low sometimes." You inspect and find a small oil leak at a pipe union, with light chafing on the pipe. Write a tech-log defect entry of five to seven lines. State the symptom, the location, and the condition. Separate your observation from any assumption, and mark the unit unserviceable.

Remember your reader. Another mechanic, or an inspector, must understand your entry months later without asking you. So write plainly, record only the facts, and never hide a defect to close a job faster.

Approach this composition as authentic professional communication, not merely a language exercise. Imagine an inspector reviewing your entry independently, several months after the original occurrence. Your description must therefore communicate the symptom, location, and condition without additional clarification. Because ambiguity generates unnecessary investigation, disciplined precision genuinely accelerates the eventual rectification.

Model answer
Pilot report: number two engine oil pressure looks low at times.
Symptom: the oil pressure has read low intermittently in the cruise.
Location: number two engine, at the oil pipe union.
Condition: the low reading appears during the cruise, not on the ground.
Observation: a small oil leak is present at the union. The pipe shows light chafing.
A worn seal is only an assumption, so the cause is not yet confirmed.
Action: the engine is unserviceable. Do not release the aircraft until we rectify the leak.
