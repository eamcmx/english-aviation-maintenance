2 · First-language interference — Latvian & Russian

These errors are predictable, not careless. Knowing the source lets you recast quickly and consistently.

LV + RUDropped articles

Error: "AMM lists every task." · "Read effectivity first." Fix: "The AMM lists…" / "Read the effectivity…"

Why: Neither Latvian nor Russian has a definite/indefinite article system. Learners map their own zero-article pattern onto English, so "the" is omitted before manual names and specialist nouns. Because the documents are specific (the AMM, the IPC, the tech log), the definite article is almost always required. Drill it as a fixed collocation ("the AMM"), not as a grammar rule.

LV + RUMissing third-person -s

Error: "The manual list every task." · "The IPC give the part numbers." Fix: "…lists…" / "…gives…"

Why: Russian marks person and number on the verb in a way that does not line up with the English -s, and Latvian agreement patterns differ too; the single English -s is easy to lose, especially in fast speech. It is also low in salience (a quiet final consonant), so learners under-hear it. This is the single highest-value drill in the unit — practise it in full sentences with singular subjects (the AMM, the chapter, a warning).

LV + RUContinuous for a permanent fact

Error: "The chapter is covering the landing gear." Fix: "The chapter covers the landing gear."

Why: The aspect systems differ from English. Learners over-extend the present continuous to states and permanent facts, partly by analogy with "-ing = now". Teach the contrast explicitly: continuous for something happening right now ("I am removing the wheel"); simple for how the manual set is always organised ("the AMM lists every task").

RUConfusing "make / do" and preposition choice

Error: "make a work order", "comply the directive". Fix: "raise a work order", "comply with the directive".

Why: Russian often maps a single verb onto both English "make" and "do", and prepositions rarely map one-to-one. Teach the unit collocations as whole chunks (raise / embody / comply with / consult / follow) rather than as free verb + noun choices.

LV + RUPronunciation of spoken figures

Error: "thirty" vs "thirteen"; "forty-one" run together; "four-oh-one" mis-said. Fix: stress and the final "-teen/-ty" contrast.

Why: The "-teen"/"-ty" contrast and the English way of reading numbers group by group (thirty-two, forty-one, oh-one) are unfamiliar. Because the whole unit turns on numbers, drill them aloud — this is exactly what the numerical quiz items test.

3 · Extension activities

  • Chapter sprint. Call out a system (brakes, navigation display, cabin lights); pairs race to name the likely ATA chapter and page block, then say a full present-simple sentence ("Chapter 32 covers the brakes").
  • Find-the-fault dictation. Read a short handover note aloud with three planted errors (a dropped article, a missing -s, a continuous-for-fact); learners write it and correct it.
  • Document relay. Give each group one document (work order, AMM, IPC, tech log, MEL, SB, AD). They explain in two sentences what it does and which document it points to — rebuilds the Dialogue 3 web.
  • Avionics transfer. Repeat the ATA-number builder for a wiring or BITE task in chapter 34 (navigation), so mechanics and avionics technicians both see the system work.
  • STE rewrite. Give a wordy, ambiguous instruction; pairs rewrite it in Simplified Technical English (short sentences, one idea, present simple, approved terms).
  • Effectivity check. Provide three tail numbers and an effectivity line; learners decide, in full sentences, which aircraft the task applies to and which must use a different task card.

4 · CEFR B2 speaking rubric — role-plays

Use while the pairs work; listen, do not interrupt, feed back afterwards. Descriptors are tuned to this unit's documentation talk.

CriterionAbove B2Solid B2 (target)Approaching B2Below B2
Range Uses the specialist terms precisely and varies structure freely; natural connectors (although, unless, as soon as). Uses most unit terms correctly and some connectors; enough range to explain where a task lives and why. Relies on a few core terms; repeats simple structures; some terms vague or avoided. Very limited vocabulary; specialist terms mostly missing or wrong; cannot sustain the task.
Accuracy Present simple and -s consistently correct; articles reliable; errors rare and self-repaired. Present simple mostly correct; the -s and articles slip occasionally but do not block meaning. Frequent -s and article errors; some continuous-for-fact; meaning still usually clear. Errors are frequent and impede meaning; the listener often has to reconstruct.
Fluency Speaks smoothly at a natural pace; hesitation only to plan complex content. Keeps going with only occasional pausing; some reformulation, but the flow holds. Noticeable pausing and false starts; flow breaks on the specialist content. Halting, word-by-word; long pauses stall the exchange.
Interaction Manages the exchange fully — asks precise questions, checks, confirms, hands over naturally. Asks and answers relevantly; takes turns well; can start and respond to questions about the task. Responds but rarely initiates; needs prompting to ask the mentor/trainee questions. Cannot sustain the interaction; relies on the partner to carry it.
Coherence Explanation is clearly ordered (effectivity → warnings → cautions → steps; work order → AMM → IPC → tech log) and easy to follow. A clear line of thought; the documentation order is mostly logical and linked. Some organisation, but the order of documents or the safety hierarchy is muddled in places. Ideas are disconnected; the listener cannot follow where the task is or why.
Quick mark: a solid-B2 pass on Card 1/3 means naming the chapter and page block and checking the effectivity, warnings and cautions in order; on Card 2/4 it means explaining the AMM–IPC cross-reference and where the job is recorded — all in accurate present simple.
