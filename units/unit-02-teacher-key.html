First-language interference — Latvian & Russian

Both first languages differ from English in exactly the two places this unit stresses: articles and the negative imperative. Understanding why the error happens lets you correct the cause, not just the symptom.

1 · The missing article LV RU

Neither Latvian nor Russian has articles at all — there is no word for a or the. Definiteness is instead carried by word order, case, or context. So when a learner produces "Remove access panel" the article is not "forgotten"; the learner's grammar simply has no slot for it. The fix — "Remove the access panel" — must be taught as a fixed habit for task cards: every specific, known noun takes the. Drill it until it is automatic, because under pressure the L1 pattern returns first.

2 · The negative imperative LV RU

English builds a negative command with the auxiliary do not + base verb — a structure both languages lack. Russian negates with the particle ne directly before the verb (ne trogaj = "not touch"), and Latvian with the prefix ne- attached to the verb (neaiztiec). Transferred to English, this yields "Not touch the connector" or the mis-ordered "touch not / reuse not". Teach the frame explicitly: Do not + base verb, with do not always before the verb. This is the single most common error on task-card English.

3 · The imported infinitive to RU

Russian modal-like expressions take an infinitive (dolzhen delat'), and learners over-generalise the English pattern "want to do", producing "You should to inspect" — a double fault (an intrusive subject and a stray to). The card wants neither: "Inspect the seal." Latvian speakers make this less often but still add the subject you. Cue the class: a step has no subject and no to — it starts with the verb.

4 · Perfective vs -s ending LV RU

Both languages inflect the verb heavily, so learners sometimes carry a spurious ending into English — "Inspects the weld" — treating the imperative like a conjugated form. Remind them the English imperative is the bare, uninflected base verb: no -s, no ending, ever.

Correction strategy: for articles and negatives, prefer recast + repeat — say the correct form and have the learner say it back — over long explanation. Adults fix these faster by re-producing the correct pattern many times than by grammar talk. Name the L1 cause once, then drill.

CEFR B2 speaking rubric — the role-plays

Use while circulating during the four role-play cards (slide 17). Five criteria, three bands each. B2 is the target band; below-B2 and above-B2 are shown so you can place a learner and give a next step.

CriterionApproaching (B1)Target (B2)Strong (B2+ → C1)
Range Relies on a few memorised phrases; reaches for basic words; avoids the specialist terms. Uses the unit's task-card vocabulary and collocations (inspect for, torque to, fit new lockwire) accurately across the walkthrough. Varies phrasing freely; paraphrases a term when needed; adds connectors (although, unless, as soon as) naturally.
Accuracy Frequent article and negative-imperative errors that sometimes obscure the instruction. Controls the imperative, articles and negatives; errors occur but rarely block meaning; self-corrects some. High control; slips are rare and immediately repaired; handles conditions (if/when) correctly.
Fluency Halting; long pauses to search for words; the confirmation loop stalls. Keeps the read → confirm → query rhythm going at a workable pace; pauses to plan, not to panic. Smooth and steady; pausing is for emphasis or thought, not language retrieval.
Interaction Waits to be prompted; confirms with a bare "yes"; does not query unclear steps. Confirms in full ("Step two is complete"), queries an unclear step, and responds to the partner's queries. Manages the exchange — checks understanding, invites confirmation, and negotiates ambiguity actively.
Coherence Steps out of order or run together; no signposting. Follows the sequence and signposts it (first, then, next); links a defect to its consequence. Organises the whole walkthrough clearly, flagging conditions and outcomes so the partner never loses the thread.
Quick capture: jot one letter per criterion (R A F I C) with −/=/+ against the B2 band, plus two words of evidence. Feed back two strengths and one fix per learner — never more; over-correction kills fluency practice.

Extension activities

For fast finishers, mixed-ability groups, or a second session on the same material.

1 · Write a task card (STE). In pairs, students write a real six-step card for a simple everyday job (change a wheel, replace a light unit) using only the imperative and Simplified Technical English. Swap cards; the other pair performs the steps literally and flags any ambiguous line. Reinforces the writing task and the query behaviour.
2 · Defect debate. Give a scenario: a mechanic wants to sign off a step with a minor unrecorded defect to close the job before shift end. Half the group argues why the step cannot be signed off; the other half plays time pressure. Targets Outcome 3 and the modal/conditional language (unless, until, provided that).
3 · WARNING / CAUTION / NOTE hunt. Hand out (or project) a real AMM page or task card. Students find and classify every warning box and justify each: who or what is at risk? Sharpens Outcome 2 on authentic documentation.
4 · Numbers dictation. Read a short handover like the voicemail at natural pace. Students transcribe only the figures (stand, reference number, torque, hours). Rebuilds spoken-number comprehension — a real weak spot at B2.
5 · Genre sort. Give short extracts from a work order, task card, SB, AD, MEL and tech log with the titles removed. Students identify each genre and state its purpose. Extends the vocabulary bank into document literacy (links to quiz R01–R05 and V04).
