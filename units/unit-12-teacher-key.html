Unit staging & timing (90 minutes)

The unit is a full troubleshooting simulation. Learners move from a BITE code, through the WDM and a continuity check, to isolating the LRU and writing the result. Keep the pace brisk; the reading and role-plays carry the back half of the lesson.

Latvian & Russian interference — why each error happens

Latvian and Russian share two features that shape these learners' English: neither language has articles, and neither uses an infinitive marker after a modal. Both facts surface exactly in troubleshooting talk.

LVRU 1 · Dropped articles

"Measure pin voltage." → "Measure the pin voltage." Latvian and Russian have no a/the, so learners omit them, especially before a known, specific thing (the connector, the harness, the reading). This is the single most frequent slip in the unit. Drill it as a fixed habit: "known thing → the".

RU 2 · Missing article vs over-use of "the"

Russian speakers sometimes over-correct once taught articles, inserting the before uncountable readings ("the resistance is high" is fine, but "do the continuity check" should be "do a continuity check" on first mention). Point out first-mention a vs known the.

LVRU 3 · "must to" after a modal

"You must to disconnect it." → "You must disconnect it." Latvian (jums jāatvieno) and Russian (вы должны отсоединить) both attach a to-like form, so learners carry it over. Rule: after must / can / should / will, use the bare base verb.

LVRU 4 · "At first" for step one

"At first, run the BITE." → "First, run the BITE." Both first languages use one adverb (vispirms / сначала) for both "firstly" and "at first", so learners reach for at first, which in English means "in the beginning, but it changed later". Teach the contrast directly.

RU 5 · Present continuous with a dropped auxiliary or the bare "-ing"

"I am check the harness." / "I checking now." → "I am checking the harness." Russian has no be + -ing aspect, so the form is assembled incompletely. Narrating live actions in the role-play is the best practice for it.

LVRU 6 · Verb–noun collocation (make vs do)

"Make a continuity check." → "Do / run a continuity check." Both languages use one verb (darīt / делать) where English splits make and do. Teach the fixed pairs: run the BITE, do a check, read a code, set the meter.

RU 7 · Consonant clusters & word stress in the terms

Watch pronunciation of splice, shield, bonding and intermittent (stress on the third syllable: in-ter-MIT-tent). Russian speakers may soften final consonants; model and chorus.

Extension activities

  • Second fault card. Give a new BITE code and a different symptom (e.g. a short circuit between pins, or a ground fault to the airframe). Learners narrate the isolation from scratch, in the correct order.
  • Board the flowchart. In pairs, draw the diagnostic flow (BITE → WDM → disconnect → continuity → repair → re-test → log), labelling every arrow with a sequencing word.
  • STE re-write. Hand out a wordy, passive maintenance note and have learners re-write it in Simplified Technical English: one idea per sentence, the active voice, approved words only.
  • MEL decision. Discuss when the fault could be deferred under the MEL (category C, up to three days) and when it must not — a good bridge to regulatory reading.
  • Telephone gap. One learner reads the shift-handover message aloud once; the partner, who cannot see it, must reconstruct the fault code, connector and next action. Trains listening for spoken figures.
  • Pronunciation clinic. Minimal-pair and stress drill on the seven flagged terms above.

CEFR B2 speaking rubric — the role-plays

Use for the paired simulation and the shift-handover / pilot cards. Five criteria; four bands. B2 is the target band ("At B2"). Give each learner a profile across the five rows rather than a single number.

CriterionAbove B2At B2 (target)Approaching B2Below B2
Range Uses the full term set and varied structures precisely and flexibly. Handles the bank terms and sequencing structures; enough range to narrate each step clearly. Core terms present but limited variety; leans on a few structures. Very restricted; misses key terms, so the step is unclear.
Accuracy Errors rare and self-repaired; articles and modals consistently correct. Good control; slips (articles, must to) do not block meaning and are often self-corrected. Frequent L1-driven errors that sometimes obscure the instruction. Errors dense enough that the partner cannot follow safely.
Fluency Smooth, natural pace; hesitation only for genuine thought. Keeps going at a steady pace; pauses to plan a step but does not stall. Noticeable hesitation; the narration stops and starts. Long pauses; cannot sustain the running commentary.
Interaction Leads and follows fluently; asks for and confirms readings, checks safety unprompted. Asks for each reading, confirms steps, and responds to the partner's cues. Responds but rarely initiates; needs prompting to confirm or query. Little real exchange; talks over or ignores the partner.
Coherence Steps flow in a clear order with varied, well-placed connectors. Uses sequencing words correctly so the order of the fault-find is easy to follow. Order mostly clear but connectors are few or repetitive. Steps out of order or unlinked; the listener loses the thread.

Quick decision: a learner is "At B2" overall if a partner who cannot see the diagram could carry out the fault-find from the spoken narration alone.
