2 · Latvian & Russian interference — why each error happens

The grammar focus is the imperative with safety warnings. Three predictable errors come straight from the first languages. Knowing the why lets you correct the cause, not just the symptom.

2.1 Dropping the auxiliary "do" in negatives

✗ Not stand near the flaps.  →  ✓ Do not stand near the flaps.

Latvian forms the negative imperative with a single prefix on the verb — nestāviet ("do-not-stand"). Russian uses one particle before the verb — не стойте. Neither language has a separate auxiliary, so the English "do" feels redundant and gets dropped. Fix: teach "Do not + base verb" as a fixed safety formula and drill it until automatic.

2.2 "No + verb" as a blanket negator

✗ No touch the actuator.  →  ✓ Do not touch the actuator.

Learners map Latvian nē / Russian нет ("no") onto every negative, producing "No + verb". In both L1s "no/nē/нет" answers a question; it does not negate a command. Fix: contrast "No." (an answer) with "Do not …" (a command). Flag it whenever it appears.

2.3 Keeping the subject pronoun

✗ You stand clear now!  →  ✓ Stand clear now!

Russian and Latvian imperatives carry person in the verb ending, and the pronoun is often kept for emphasis (ты стой / tu stāvi). Transferred to English it sounds like an accusation, not an order. Fix: "An English order has no subject — drop 'you'." Note that "you" reappears only for contrast ("You watch the left flap; I read the manual").

2.4 Other errors to expect

  • "must to" + verb (✗ we must to run) — L1 infinitive marker transferred; modals take the bare infinitive. (See E6.3.)
  • Missing article (✗ raise snag) — neither L1 has articles, so "a/the" is routinely omitted. (E6.7.)
  • Dropped dummy subject (✗ is a fail) — L1 allows subjectless clauses; English needs "it is". (E6.5.)
  • Subject–verb agreement (✗ flaps is) — plural agreement is unstable under pressure. (E6.4.)

3 · Extension activities

  • Reverse the fault (speaking). Rerun Card 2, but this time the test passes. Partners must produce the full call-and-confirm sequence ending "That meets the pass criteria — sign it serviceable." Trains the positive-outcome register.
  • Two-minute handover (fluency). Students leave a 30-second voicemail like Track 4 for a different fault (e.g. a late limit switch). Record on a phone, swap, and note-take the five facts.
  • STE rewrite (writing). Give a wordy real-style snag ("It seems that possibly the left flap might not be moving quite as far as it should…") and have students rewrite it in Simplified Technical English — short, one idea per sentence.
  • Avionics swap (vocabulary). Apply the same language to a BITE-driven avionics test (e.g. a rudder travel test). Which terms transfer? Which new ones appear (channel, fault code)?
  • Grammar sprint. Board ten "You must not / You should …" prompts; teams race to convert to imperatives. First correct set wins.
